The Normal Distribution

Gauss's bell curve — the shape of measurement itself. Area under the curve IS probability, and Excel computes every area you'll ever need.

1 · Where it came from

Gauss didn’t invent the bell curve — Abraham de Moivre sketched it first in 1733 while working out gambling odds. But Carl Friedrich Gauss made it famous. In the early 1800s he was tracking asteroid orbits and needed a way to handle measurement errors. His insight: errors cluster around the true value in a predictable, symmetric pattern. That pattern is the normal distribution.

Germany put Gauss on the 10-mark banknote — with the bell curve printed beside his face. The man is the distribution. The curve then turned up everywhere honest measurement happens: heights, fill weights, delivery times, order sizes — anything produced by many small independent influences added together (that’s the Central Limit Theorem’s doing).

2 · The mathematics

f(x)  =  1σ √(2π)  ·  e−½ ((x−μ)/σ)²
μ (mu) — the mean: where the peak sits · σ (sigma) — the standard deviation: how wide the bell spreads · x — any value on the number line · e, π — the constants 2.71828… and 3.14159…

Plain English: how dense is probability at this value x, given the center and the spread? The curve never touches zero, is perfectly symmetric, and its mean = median = mode.

Mean = μ   ·   Variance = σ²   ·   z = x − μσ
Signature — the empirical rule: ~68% of values fall within ±1σ of the mean, ~95% within ±2σ, ~99.7% within ±3σ. If your data badly violates this, it isn’t normal.
The area IS the probability. The curve’s total area is exactly 1. Every probability question — P(X > 1350), P(1100 < X < 1300) — is an area question. Excel’s job is computing those areas so you never integrate by hand.

3 · What it looks like — drag it around

area ABOVE x (shaded):  =1-NORM.DIST(1350, 1200, 80, TRUE)  → 0.0304
The green readout is what Excel should return. Type the formula, match the number.
The three question patterns (from your Day-7 classroom material):  Left tail P(X < a) = NORM.DIST(a, μ, σ, TRUE) · Right tail P(X > a) = 1 - NORM.DIST(a, μ, σ, TRUE) · Between P(a < X < b) = NORM.DIST(b,…) - NORM.DIST(a,…). And the reverse gear: NORM.DIST goes value → probability; NORM.INV goes probability → value.

4 · Excel — run it in class

Scenario: A Boca lighting wholesaler ships orders averaging μ = 1200 g with σ = 80 g. Shipping charges an oversize fee above 1350 g. Set up:

celltype thismeaningyou should see

B7=NORM.DIST(1300,B1,B2,TRUE)-NORM.DIST(1100,B1,B2,TRUE)area between two values (P(1100 < X < 1300))0.7887
B8=NORM.INV(0.95,B1,B2)reverse question: the weight only 5% of orders exceed1331.6
The TRUE argument means cumulative — area from −∞ up to x. That’s why “above x” is always 1 − NORM.DIST(…, TRUE), and “between a and b” is the difference of two cumulatives. FALSE gives the curve’s height instead — almost never what a business question wants.
Business read: about 3 orders in 100 pay the fee. If the fee is \$12, expected fee cost ≈ 0.0304 × \$12 ≈ \$0.36 per order — now it’s a pricing decision.

5 · Does real data fit?

How to check fit: histogram your data, overlay the normal curve built from the sample’s own mean and SD, and look: one symmetric hump, tails dying off, ~95% inside mean ± 2 SD. Skewed or twin-peaked data fails the eye test before any formal test.

6 · When to reach for it — and when not

Reach for Normal when…Do NOT assume Normal when…
Continuous measurements from many small influences (weights, times, dimensions) Data is counts (that’s Poisson/Binomial territory)
Histogram is one symmetric humpData is skewed (incomes, house prices, wait times)
Averages of many observations (the CLT delivers normality of means) Sample is small and σ unknown (the t-distribution exists for exactly this)

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. Why is the normal the right model for AVERAGE order value across thousands of orders even though individual orders are skewed?

The single most load-bearing fact in the course: raw data keeps its shape, but MEANS of large samples go normal. That's why mean-based inference works at all

Q2. Which quantity should you NOT model as normal?

Heavy skew in RAW individual values defies the bell; averages and error terms embrace it. Know which side of the CLT your quantity sits on

Q3. HOW does the normal model earn its keep on a dashboard?

The bell converts raw numbers into 'how unusual': z-scores, percentiles, tail chances. Trends are regression's job; outliers are found, not removed, by z
