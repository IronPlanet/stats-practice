The Kruskal–Wallis H Test

ANOVA on the ranks — Kruskal and Wallis’s 1952 answer to “do three or more groups differ?” when the groups are skewed, unequal, small, or full of outliers.

1 · Where it came from

1952. William Kruskal at the University of Chicago and W. Allen Wallis — later the economist who ran the wartime Statistical Research Group at Columbia and would become an Under-Secretary of State — publish “Use of Ranks in One-Criterion Variance Analysis” in the Journal of the American Statistical Association. The title says it all: take Fisher’s analysis of variance, which compares three or more group means and needs bell-shaped, equal-spread groups, and run it on the ranks instead of the values. Wilcoxon had done this for two groups in 1945; Kruskal and Wallis extended it to any number and worked out the referee: a χ² curve with k − 1 degrees of freedom.

The payoff is the same as for every rank test. Pool all the observations, number them 1 to N from smallest to largest, and ask whether one group has grabbed the high numbers. A wild value becomes rank N and nothing more; a skewed group is just a group whose ranks lean one way. No group needs to be Normal, and the groups needn’t share a spread — the two assumptions that most often break one-way ANOVA on business data (revenues by region, wait times by branch, ratings by plan).

2 · The mathematics

The question it answers. Three or more independent groups. Does at least one of them tend to have larger values than the others? It replaces one-way ANOVA when the groups are skewed, unequal in spread, small, or ordinal.
Step 1   pool all N observations; rank 1 … N (ties share the average rank)
Step 2   for each group g: ng observations, Rg = sum of its ranks, R̄g = Rg/ng its mean rank
Step 3   H = 12N(N+1) Σg ng R̄g2 − 3(N+1)     (equivalently: H = (N − 1) · Σ ng(R̄g − R̄)2Σ (ri − R̄)2 — ANOVA’s SSbetween/SStotal on the ranks)
Step 4   referee χ²(k − 1); p = P(χ² ≥ H). With ties: divide H by 1 − Σ(t³ − t)/(N³ − N).
H0: all k groups come from the same distribution — every group’s mean rank ≈ (N+1)/2 · H1: at least one group tends to be higher or lower · H = 0 when every mean rank equals the overall mean rank; it grows as groups pull apart · the χ² approximation is good when every ng ≥ 5 (exact tables exist for tiny groups).

Why χ²?

Ranks 1 … N have a known mean (N+1)/2 and variance (N² − 1)/12 before you look at any data. Under H0 each group’s mean rank is an average of ng draws from that fixed population, so it is approximately Normal with a known SE. H is the sum of the squared standardised deviations of the k mean ranks — a sum of k squared z-scores with one constraint (they average to (N+1)/2) — which is a χ² with k − 1 degrees of freedom. The 12/(N(N+1)) is that variance turned inside out.

After a significant H — which groups?

H says “something differs”, not where. Follow with Dunn’s test (pairwise z on mean ranks with a Bonferroni or Holm correction) or pairwise Mann–Whitney tests at α / (number of pairs). Three groups → three pairs → α = 0.0167 each. The rank analogue of Tukey HSD.

5 · Does the test fit? — the assumptions

Ordinal or numeric outcome—Categorical outcome → χ² test of independence
Similar shapes across groups if you want to say “medians differ”Overlay histograms / boxplotsDifferent shapes → say “tends to be larger”, not “median differs” (same caveat as Mann–Whitney)
ng ≥ 5 for the χ² refereeCountTiny groups → exact permutation p (software)
Power. When ANOVA’s assumptions do hold, Kruskal–Wallis has about 95 % of its power — you lose almost nothing. When they fail, ANOVA can lose all of its power to a single outlier (the lab above). That asymmetry is the argument for the rank test on business data.

6 · When to reach for it — and when not

Kruskal–Wallis when…Something else when…
3+ independent groups; skewed, outlier-prone or ordinal outcome (wait times, revenue per account, ratings)Exactly 2 groups → Mann–Whitney (same test, k = 2)
Group spreads clearly unequal and groups smallGroups roughly Normal, similar spreads, n ≥ 30 each → one-way ANOVA (keeps units, gives CIs, Tukey)
You want a verdict that one bad day cannot flipTwo factors or a covariate → two-way ANOVA / regression; no clean rank equivalent
Follow-up is a ranking of groups (which branch is worst)The business needs “how many minutes” with a CI → add medians + Hodges–Lehmann, or bootstrap the means
Three mistakes. (1) Stopping at H — a significant H without pairwise follow-ups is unfinished. (2) Pairwise Mann–Whitney at 0.05 each — three tests at 0.05 is a 14 % family-wise false-alarm rate; divide α. (3) Calling it a “test of medians” when the shapes differ — it is a test of whether one group tends to be higher.
Reporting line. “Kruskal–Wallis H(2) = 16.3, p = 0.0003, ε² = 0.81; medians 17 / 23 / 30 min; all pairwise Mann–Whitney p < 0.0167.” Then what it cannot say: why branch 3 is slower, and whether the 90-minute day was a one-off.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. Three regions’ monthly revenue per account are right-skewed with a few whale accounts each. You want to know whether any region differs. First choice?

Skew + whales break ANOVA’s Normal/equal-variance assumptions and let single accounts drive F. Ranks bound each whale to one position. Follow a significant H with corrected pairwise tests.

Q2. Kruskal–Wallis: H = 7.9, df = 2, p = 0.019. What can you conclude, and what can’t you?

H is an omnibus test like ANOVA’s F. Which pairs, and the size in real units, come from Dunn/Mann–Whitney follow-ups and the group medians.

Q3. With N = 21 in three groups of 7, group 3’s mean rank is 17.9 and the overall mean rank is 11. Roughly what does 17.9 say?

Mean rank has no units; it is a position. 17.9 of 21 means most of group 3 ranks above most of the other fourteen observations.

Q4. The same 12 customers rate three service tiers (each customer rates all three). Which test?

Kruskal–Wallis needs independent groups. Each customer contributing three related ratings is a blocked / repeated-measures design → Friedman.
