Power & Sample Size

Why a study misses a real effect, how many observations it would take not to, and why computing power afterwards proves nothing.

1 · Where it came from

Jerzy Neyman and Egon Pearson spent the late 1920s arguing with R. A. Fisher about what a statistical test is for. Fisher's p-value measured evidence against a null. Neyman and Pearson asked the harder question: a test can be wrong in two directions, so how do you design one that is wrong as rarely as possible in the direction you care about? Their 1933 paper named the second error and the quantity that controls it.

Forty years later Jacob Cohen found that the psychology literature had quietly ignored it. Surveying published studies, he showed that a typical experiment had roughly a coin-flip chance of detecting the effect it was looking for — and that authors were reporting “no significant difference” as though it meant “no difference”. His 1962 survey and the standardised effect size that carries his name are the reason power is now part of designing a study rather than an afterthought.

2 · The mathematics

α = P(reject H₀ | H₀ true)      β = P(fail to reject H₀ | H₁ true)      power = 1 − β
α is the false-alarm rate and you choose it. β is the miss rate and you do not — it falls out of the effect size, the sample size and α together. Power is the probability that a real effect of a stated size actually shows up as significant.
d = x̄1 − x̄2spooled      spooled = √ (n1−1)s12 + (n2−1)s22n1 + n2 − 2       dz = d̄sd
Cohen's d expresses a difference in standard deviations, so it is comparable across studies and across units. For paired designs use dz — the mean difference over the SD of the differences, which is a different and usually much larger number. Cohen's 0.2 / 0.5 / 0.8 labels are conventions he later regretted publishing; a “small” effect on a large book of business can be worth more than a “large” one on a small book.
n per group ≈ 2(z1−α/2 + zpower)2d2
Read the d2 in the denominator. Sample size scales with one over the square of the effect. Halving the effect you want to detect roughly quadruples the sample. Detecting d = 0.86 at 80% power needs about 22 per group; detecting half of it needs about 86. This single fact decides more study designs than any other in the course.

3 · Watch α and β trade off

Two curves: the test statistic when there is no effect, and when the effect is real. The critical value sits where α says it does. Everything of the right-hand curve that falls left of that line is a miss.

power = —  ·  β (miss rate) = —  ·  n for 80% power = —  ·  —
Three things worth doing. Drop n and watch the right-hand curve slide left until most of it is a miss. Raise α and watch power rise — you buy detection by accepting more false alarms. And set d small with n small: power falls toward α itself, which is the state most under-powered studies are actually in.

Twelve climate-controlled storage units and twelve standard ones, monthly revenue per unit. Climate in A2:A13, standard in B2:B13.

This result is significant and the study was still under-powered. p = 0.0480 clears 0.05, so the difference is real enough to report. But the design had only 55% power against the effect that turned up — close to a coin flip. Run this same study again with twelve fresh units per group and it would fail to reach significance about 45 times in 100. Nothing is wrong with the finding; what is wrong is the design's reliability, and the p-value says nothing about it. Detecting d = 0.86 at 80% power needs 22 per group, not 12.
Look at the MDE and you can see the luck. At n = 12 the smallest effect this design could reliably detect is d = 1.14, and the effect present was only d = 0.86 — smaller than the design's own detection threshold. It reached significance anyway because sampling noise happened to fall the helpful way. That is not a criticism of the analyst; it is the reason power is computed before collecting data rather than after.
Now make the effect realistic. Suppose the true difference had been a modest d = 0.30. The same twelve-per-group design would have had 11.4% power — missing a real difference about 89 times out of 100, barely better than the 5% false-alarm rate itself. Detecting d = 0.30 at 80% power needs 175 per group. A design is never good or bad on its own, only relative to the effect you are hunting.

5 · Minimum detectable effect — the question to ask first

Usually n is fixed before you arrive: this is the data that exists. Then the useful question reverses. Instead of “how many do I need?”, ask “what is the smallest effect this design could reliably find?”

MDE = (z1−α/2 + zpower) · √ 2/n

Compare the MDE to what the business cares about. If the smallest change worth acting on is a 4% lift and your design can only detect a 15% lift, the study cannot answer the question — and you know that before collecting anything. That conversation is cheap in advance and impossible afterwards.

6 · Design beats sample size: pairing

The same twelve observations, arranged two ways. An effect of d = 0.90, α = 0.05:

DesignnPowerVerdict
Independent groups12 per group60%would miss the effect most of the time
Paired / repeated measures12 pairs88%would almost always find it
Independent, matched to that power≈25 per group88%what it costs to buy the same power without pairing
Pairing removes between-unit variation from the denominator. Measuring the same stores before and after, rather than different stores, deletes the store-to-store spread that was drowning the signal. It is the cheapest power increase available, and it is a design decision — unavailable once the data is collected.

7 · Three ways power gets misused

1. “Not significant” reported as “no difference”. A non-significant result from an under-powered test is not evidence of absence — it is absence of evidence. The honest sentence names the power: “this design had 11% power against a d = 0.30 effect, so a null result does not rule one out.”
2. Retrospective power. Computing power from the effect you observed is circular: it is a one-to-one function of the p-value you already have, so it adds no information. It cannot rescue a null result and it cannot justify a design after the fact. The effect size must be specified before the data is seen — from prior studies, a pilot, or the smallest difference that would change the decision.
3. Type III error — the right answer to the wrong question. A test can be perfectly executed, adequately powered and correctly interpreted, and still fail, because it answered a question the decision did not ask. Proving climate control raises revenue per unit does not tell you whether it pays for its own installation cost. There is no statistical fix — only asking the question first.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. A study with 12 per group finds p = 0.34 and concludes “climate control makes no difference to revenue”. The design had 11% power against the effect size management cares about. What is the correct conclusion?

With 11% power the test misses a real effect roughly nine times in ten. A null result from such a design tells you almost nothing — absence of evidence, not evidence of absence. The honest report states the power alongside the p-value.

Q2. You can detect an effect of d = 0.80 with about 25 per group. Roughly what is needed for d = 0.40?

n scales with 1/d², so halving the effect roughly quadruples the sample: 25 × 4 ≈ 100 (the formula gives 98). Doubling to 50 is the intuitive answer and it is wrong — that linear instinct is what wrecks study budgets.

Q3. After a non-significant result, an analyst computes power using the effect size that was observed and reports 19%. What does this add?

Retrospective power is a one-to-one function of the p-value already in hand, so it carries no independent information and cannot justify a design after the fact. A useful power calculation names the effect size worth detecting before the data is seen.

Q4. A test proves climate-controlled units earn significantly more per month. The decision was whether to spend $180,000 converting the building. What kind of error is possible here even if the statistics are flawless?

The test answered “do they earn more?” The decision needed “do they earn enough more to repay $180,000, and over what period?” No sample size or α protects against this; only framing the question before testing does.
