The Poisson Distribution

Counts of rare events per window — born from gambling math, proven on Prussian horse-kick deaths, used on every queue you'll ever staff.

1 · Where it came from

Siméon Denis Poisson published his distribution in 1837 to model rare events. The story that made it famous came sixty years later: in 1898 Ladislaus Bortkiewicz analyzed how many Prussian cavalry soldiers were kicked to death by horses each year — about 0.7 per corps per year, rare and random. Poisson’s formula fit the death counts almost perfectly.

That’s the mic drop: the Poisson models the count of rare events in a fixed window. Server crashes per month. Customer complaints per day. Insurance claims per quarter. Café walk-ins per ten minutes. If the event is rare-ish, independent, and arrives at a roughly constant average rate — Poisson.

2 · The mathematics

P(X = k)  =  λk e−λk!
λ (lambda) — the average number of events per window · k — the exact count you’re asking about (0, 1, 2, …) · k! — k factorial · e — 2.71828…

Plain English: if events average λ per window, what’s the chance of seeing exactly k in one window? One parameter runs the whole show.

Mean = λ   ·   Variance = λ
Signature — mean equals variance. This is the Poisson fingerprint. If your count data has mean 4.2 and variance 4.1, Poisson is knocking. Mean 4.2 with variance 11? Something else is going on (clumping, mixed rates).
Binomial connection: when n is large (>20) and p is small (<0.05), the binomial collapses into a Poisson with λ = np — one number instead of two, and easier arithmetic. Rare defects in big batches live here.

3 · What it looks like — drag λ

P(X > k) — orange bars:  =1-POISSON.DIST(8, 5, TRUE)  → 0.0681
Bars, not a curve — counts are whole numbers. Watch the shape: skewed at small λ, nearly bell-shaped by λ ≈ 10.
The three question patterns: Exactly k = POISSON.DIST(k, λ, FALSE) · At most k = POISSON.DIST(k, λ, TRUE) · More than k = 1 - POISSON.DIST(k, λ, TRUE). Every Poisson exam question is one of these three wearing a costume.

B7=POISSON.DIST(3,B1,TRUE)P(X ≤ 3) — a slow window0.2650
TRUE vs FALSE: TRUE sums the bars from 0 up to k (cumulative); FALSE is the single bar P(X = k exactly). “More than k” is always 1 − POISSON.DIST(k, λ, TRUE) — note it’s more than 8, so the 8 bar itself stays in the “copes” pile.
Business read: the barista is overwhelmed in ~7% of windows — about 4 windows in a 10-hour day. Now “do we need a second barista at peak?” has a number attached.

5 · Does real data fit?

How to check fit: compute the sample mean AND variance of your counts. Mean ≈ variance is the entry ticket. Then overlay the Poisson bars built from the sample mean and eye-check the histogram.

6 · When to reach for it — and when not

Reach for Poisson when…Do NOT use Poisson when…
Counting events per fixed window (time, area, batch)The count has a hard upper limit n (that’s Binomial: k out of n trials)
Events are independent at a steady average rateEvents clump (one complaint triggers ten more) — variance will exceed the mean
Mean ≈ variance in your sampleThe rate drifts within the window (lunch rush ≠ 3 p.m. lull) — model windows separately

7 · Where it comes from — the Poisson limit theorem

The Poisson is not a separate invention. It is what the binomial becomes when the trials get numerous and each one gets rare, with the average holding still. Formally: as n grows and p shrinks with np = λ fixed, the binomial converges to the Poisson.

Binomial(n, p)  ⟶  Poisson(λ)    as   n → ∞,   p → 0,   np = λ

Watch it happen. Each row below has the same mean, np = 3. The last column is the gap from the Poisson answer, P(X = 2) = 0.224042:

The practical test is whether a fixed n exists at all. Defects out of 420 units inspected has an n — use the binomial. Customer arrivals in a ten-minute window has no n: there is no list of people who could have arrived and didn't. No n means no binomial, and the Poisson is the model built for exactly that case.
This is also why the two agree on real data so often. If someone hands you a binomial with large n and small p, the Poisson will fit almost as well — and is easier to work with, since it needs one parameter instead of two.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. Support tickets arrive at about 6 per hour, independently, no fixed cap. The model is Poisson because:

Poisson's signature: counting events in a window with no natural n, steady rate, independence. A fixed customer list would push you toward binomial

Q2. Which situation BREAKS Poisson's assumptions?

Poisson needs independent events at a steady rate. Contagion/clustering (viral bursts) violates independence — real counts will be far lumpier than Poisson predicts

Q3. HOW do you use a Poisson model of 2 breakdowns/month for maintenance planning?

One number — the rate λ — generates the whole probability menu for next month's count. Costs, comparisons, and clock times are different tools' jobs
