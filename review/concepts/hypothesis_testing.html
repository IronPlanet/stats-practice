Hypothesis Testing

Fisher’s tea, Gosset’s barley, Neyman’s two mistakes, Cohen’s d — assume nothing is going on, measure your surprise, and then say how much it matters.

1 · Where it came from

Rothamsted Experimental Station, England, late 1920s. At afternoon tea a colleague, Muriel Bristol, claims she can tell whether the milk or the tea went into the cup first. Ronald Fisher designs a test on the spot: eight cups, four each way, presented in random order. If she is guessing, the chance of getting all eight right is 1 in 70 (p = 0.014). She got them all. Fisher wrote it up in The Design of Experiments (1935), and the logic — assume nothing is going on, compute how surprising the data would be, decide — is the hypothesis test.

Two other names finish the story. William Gosset, a brewer at Guinness in Dublin, needed to compare barley yields from tiny plots and derived the t-distribution (1908, published as “Student” because Guinness forbade employees from publishing). And Jerzy Neyman & Egon Pearson (1933) pointed out that a test can fail two ways — cry wolf, or miss the wolf — and that you have to choose which mistake you can afford. Jacob Cohen (1988) added the last piece: a p-value says whether an effect exists; his d says whether it matters.

2 · The mathematics

The four-step recipe. 1 State H0 (nothing is going on — the claim you try to knock down) and H1 (something is). 2 Choose α — the false-alarm rate you’ll tolerate, usually 0.05. 3 Compute the test statistic = signal ÷ noise, and the p-value = the probability of a statistic at least this extreme if H0 were true. 4 p < α → reject H0; otherwise fail to reject (never “accept”). Then report the effect size.
t  =  x̄ − μ0s / √n     (one-sample)      t  =  d̄sd / √n  (paired: test the differences)      t  =  x̄1 − x̄2SE  (two-sample)
x̄ − μ0 — the signal: how far the sample mean sits from the claimed value · s / √n — the noise: the standard error, how much sample means wobble on their own · df = n − 1 — hand the score to the t referee · two-sample SE is pooled (equal spreads) or Welch’s (unequal) — see the field guide.
95 % CI  =  x̄ ± t0.975, n−1 · s/√n        Cohen’s d  =  x̄1 − x̄2spooled        paired: dz = d̄ / sd
CI — the range of population means the data can’t rule out; if it excludes μ0, p < 0.05 (same information, better presentation) · d — the gap in standard-deviation units: 0.2 small · 0.5 medium · 0.8 large. d doesn’t shrink as n grows; p does. Significance says the difference exists; effect size says whether it matters.

The two mistakes

H0 is actually trueH0 is actually false
Reject H0Type I error — false alarm. Probability = α (you chose it)Correct. Probability = power = 1 − β
Fail to rejectCorrect (1 − α)Type II error — a miss. Probability = β
Which mistake is expensive is a business question, not a statistical one. Shipping a change that does nothing (Type I) costs the rollout; shelving a change that works (Type II) costs the benefit forever. Power rises with effect size, sample size and α — the only lever you fully control before the data arrive is n.

6 · When to reach for it — and when not

Test when…Hold off when…
You have a specific claim to knock down (“the new process is no faster”) and a sample that could knock it downThe question is “how big?” — that’s a CI and an effect size, not a p-value
The observations are independent (one number per unit; no shared days, machines or customers hiding inside)Rows share a source — 700 readings from 12 machines is 12 independent things, not 700
The design is clear: one group vs target, paired, or two groups (three+ → ANOVA)The data are small and badly skewed — use the rank-based twin
You chose α, the direction and the test before lookingYou are hunting through many comparisons for any p < 0.05 — 20 tries at α = 0.05 finds one by luck
Three sentences every result needs. The gap in real units with its CI (“6.3 s faster, CI 3.5 to 9.1”) · the test and p (“Welch t = 4.75, p = 0.0003”) · the effect size and what it means (“d = 2.0 — a large, practically important change”). A p-value alone is a rumour.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. A/B test: p = 0.001, the new checkout converts 0.1 percentage points better, d = 0.02, n = 200,000 per arm. What do you tell the product lead?

p shrinks with n; d does not. Huge samples make trivia significant. Significance says the difference exists; effect size says whether it matters.

Q2. You set α = 0.05 and run 20 independent comparisons on data where nothing is going on. Expected number of “significant” results?

α is the false-alarm rate per test: 20 × 0.05 = 1 expected false positive. Multiple comparisons need a correction (Bonferroni, Tukey) — see the ANOVA page.

Q3. A pilot with n = 6 per group finds a gap of 4 units, p = 0.19. The honest conclusion is:

Failing to reject is not accepting H₀. Low power means a real effect often goes unseen (Type II). The CI will show how wide the uncertainty is.

Q4. Same 12 branches measured before and after a software change. The paired t gives p = 0.002; a two-sample t on the same numbers gives p = 0.15. Why?

Before/after numbers travel together. Subtracting first cancels each branch’s baseline; what is left is the change and its (much smaller) wobble.
