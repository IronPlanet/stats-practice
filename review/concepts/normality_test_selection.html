Normality Testing & Test Selection

Gauss’s bell, Pearson’s skewness, Shapiro’s W — look at the shape before you pick the test, because every t-test borrows a shape it hasn’t checked.

1 · Where it came from

1809. Carl Friedrich Gauss needed to say which of many slightly-different measurements of a planet’s position to believe. His answer — errors pile up symmetrically around the truth, small ones common, big ones rare — is the bell curve, and it turned out to describe heights, measurement noise, exam scores, and almost anything that is the sum of many small independent pushes. Adolphe Quetelet (1835) found it in chest measurements of 5,738 Scottish soldiers and invented l’homme moyen, the average man. Francis Galton built a pinball board (the quincunx, 1873) that generates it from nothing but coin flips.

The catch came later. Every test in the t family borrows the bell’s shape to compute its p-value. When the data aren’t bell-shaped — skewed revenues, wait times with a long right tail, a few wild outliers — the borrowed shape is a lie and the p-value is wrong. Karl Pearson (1895) gave us skewness and kurtosis to measure the departure; Samuel Shapiro and Martin Wilk (1965) gave us the standard test for it. The discipline this page teaches is one habit: look at the shape before you choose the test.

2 · The mathematics

z  =  x − μσ      68 % of a Normal lies within ±1σ · 95 % within ±1.96σ · 99.7 % within ±3σ
Why it matters: every parametric test converts a gap into a z- or t-score and asks “how far out on the bell is this?”. If the population isn’t a bell, “far out” means something different and the test’s error rate is no longer the α you chose.

Two numbers that describe the departure

skewness  g1 = m3m23/2      excess kurtosis  g2 = m4m22 − 3      where mk = Σ (xi − x̄)kn
Skewness — sign says which tail is long (+ = long right tail, the usual case for money and time); |g1| > 1 is “clearly skewed”, > 0.5 is “noticeable”. Excess kurtosis — > 0 means heavier tails than the bell (more outliers than a Normal would give), < 0 means lighter. A Normal has both equal to 0.

Three ways to check

CheckWhat it doesRead it as
Histogram + mean vs medianEyeball the shape; the mean gets dragged toward the long tail, the median doesn’tmean ≫ median → right skew. Cheap, always do it first
Q–Q plotSorted data on the y-axis against the values a Normal would give at the same percentiles on the x-axisPoints on a straight line → Normal. A bow → skew. An S → heavy or light tails
Shapiro–Wilk W (or Jarque–Bera)W = how well the sorted data correlate with the Normal’s expected order statistics (W = 1 is a perfect bell). Jarque–Bera: JB = n/6 · (g1² + g2²/4), referee χ²(2)p < 0.05 → “not Normal”. With large n a trivial departure rejects; with tiny n nothing does — so read it with the picture, never instead of it
The escape hatch (Central Limit Theorem): the test is about the sampling distribution of the mean, not the raw data. With n ≳ 30 per group and no extreme outliers, means are close to Normal even when the data aren’t, and a t-test is fine. The danger zone is small n + skew + outliers — exactly where a rank-based test earns its keep.

5 · Test selection — the parametric / non-parametric pairs

Every parametric test has a rank-based twin that asks almost the same question without borrowing the bell. Choose by the design first (how many groups, paired or not), then by the shape.

When to reach for the parametric test — and when not

Parametric is fine when…Switch to ranks when…
Histogram roughly symmetric, Q–Q roughly straight, |skew| < 1Long tail, |skew| > 1, or the Q–Q plot bows
n ≳ 30 per group (CLT rescues the mean) and no extreme outliersSmall n and non-Normal — the combination the t-test cannot survive
You need the answer in the data’s units (a difference in means, a CI)A few outliers would otherwise decide the verdict; you want “tends to be bigger”
The data are genuinely interval (seconds, dollars)The data are ordinal (ratings 1–5, rankings) — means of ranks are meaningless anyway
Two things not to do. (1) Don’t delete outliers to make the data Normal — report them, then use a method that tolerates them. (2) Don’t run a normality test on 1,000 rows and switch to ranks because p = 0.03; at that n the t-test was fine, and the rank test throws away the units you need for the business answer.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. A sample of 15 invoice-processing times has mean 41 min, median 28 min, skewness 1.9. You want to compare it with a second office’s 15 times. Best route?

Small n and |skew| > 1 together break the t-test’s borrowed bell. The rank test asks whether one office tends to be slower and is immune to the long tail.

Q2. A Shapiro–Wilk test on 2,000 daily sales rejects normality with p = 0.01; the histogram looks like a slightly lopsided bell, skewness 0.4. What do you do about a planned t-test?

Normality tests get more sensitive as n grows, flagging departures that don’t matter. The picture and the effect size decide, not the p-value alone.

Q3. In a Q–Q plot the points sit on the line in the middle but curve upward at the right end. That pattern means:

Points above the line at the top = observed values larger than expected at those percentiles = right skew (positive g₁). The mean will sit above the median.
