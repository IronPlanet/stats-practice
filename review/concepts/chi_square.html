The χ² Tests — Goodness of Fit and Independence

Pearson’s 1900 roulette-wheel statistic — how far are your counts from the counts you expected? Two jobs, one formula, one referee curve, and Cramér’s V to say how much.

1 · Where it came from

1900. Karl Pearson, at University College London, is annoyed by a roulette wheel. Monte Carlo had published its results and Pearson wanted a rigorous way to say whether the wheel was fair — whether the counts in each pocket were further from equal than luck allows. His answer, in the Philosophical Magazine, was the χ² (“chi-squared”) statistic: for every category, take the gap between what you observed and what you expected, square it, scale it by the expected count, and add up. He then derived the curve that this sum follows when nothing is going on — the χ² distribution — so the sum could be turned into a probability. (The wheel, he concluded, was not fair; or, more likely, the newspaper had made the numbers up.)

A quarrel finished the job. Pearson got the degrees of freedom wrong for tables, and in 1922 the young Ronald Fisher corrected him: for an r × c table the referee is χ² with (r − 1)(c − 1) degrees of freedom, not rc − 1. Pearson never forgave him. Harald Cramér (1946) added the missing piece for business use — an effect size, V, so that “significant” could be separated from “large”. The test has two jobs and one formula: goodness of fit (do the counts match a pattern I expected?) and independence (are two categorical variables linked?).

2 · The mathematics

The question it answers. You sorted things into buckets and counted. Are the counts further from “nothing interesting” than chance would produce? Categories in, counts in, one number out.
χ2  =  Σcells (O − E)2E
O — observed count in a cell · E — the count you would expect if H0 were true · squaring makes misses in both directions count and big misses count a lot · dividing by E scales the miss: 5 short of 10 is a big deal, 5 short of 1,000 is noise · χ² = 0 means a perfect match; larger means further from “boring”.

Job 1 — Goodness of fit: one variable, k categories

Ei = n · pi   (pi = the share H0 predicts for category i; equal split → pi = 1/k)      df = k − 1 (minus one more for every parameter estimated from the data)
“Are complaints spread evenly over the week?” “Do our customers match the census age mix?” “Does this die land 1–6 equally?” H0: the observed shares match the predicted ones.

Job 2 — Independence (association): two variables, an r × c table

Erc = (row total) × (column total)grand total      df = (r − 1)(c − 1)
Why that E: if plan and complaining are unrelated, each plan should complain at the overall rate — its row total times the overall complaint share. H0: the row variable and the column variable are independent (knowing one tells you nothing about the other). A 2 × 2 table has df = 1; a 4 × 2 table df = 3.

The referee and its rules

χ² with df degrees of freedom: a right-skewed hump starting at 0 (it is a sum of squares, never negative) with mean = df. The p-value is the area to the right of your χ². Rule 1: every E ≥ 5 (some allow 80 % of cells ≥ 5 and none below 1) — otherwise use Fisher’s exact test (2 × 2) or merge sparse categories. Rule 2: counts, not percentages, not means. Rule 3: each unit counted once — 300 customers, not 300 visits by 40 customers.

The curve’s mean is df: a χ² of 6 is alarming with df = 1 and unremarkable with df = 6. Always quote df with the statistic.

6 · When to reach for it — and when not

χ² when…Something else when…
Two categorical variables and a table of counts (plan × churn, region × complaint type, gender × class level)One variable is numeric → t-test / ANOVA / Mann–Whitney on the numeric one, grouped by the category
One categorical variable vs a predicted share (even split, census mix, last year’s mix)Ordered categories where the trend matters (Low/Med/High) → χ² for trend, Spearman, or ordinal regression
n large enough that every E ≥ 5Small tables → Fisher’s exact test
You will report V (or the odds ratio) and the residuals alongside pYou only want a yes/no — a yes without a size is not a basis for spending money
Three mistakes. (1) Running χ² on percentages — 45 % is not a count; 45 of 100 is. (2) Reading a tiny p as a big effect: with n = 20,000, V = 0.03 is “highly significant” and worthless. (3) Saying the test shows which category drives the association or why — residuals hint at where; nothing here says why.
Reporting line. “χ²(2, n = 180) = 14.6, p = 0.0007, Cramér’s V = 0.29; Premium churned at 58 % vs 44 % overall (std. residual +1.6).” Then what it cannot say: direction of cause, and why.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. A survey reports: 62 % of Basic customers renewed, 48 % of Premium. Can you run χ² on those two numbers?

62 % of 13 customers and 62 % of 1,300 are different evidence. Recover the counts, build the 2 × 2, check E ≥ 5, then test.

Q2. 4 × 2 table, χ² = 9.1, p = 0.028, n = 800. Cramér’s V?

min(r−1, c−1) = min(3, 1) = 1. V ≈ 0.107. With n = 800 even a weak link is significant; V is what tells the Dean whether it merits investment.

Q3. 2 × 2 table with expected counts 12.5, 7.5, 4.2, 2.8. What do you do?

The χ² curve is an approximation that needs E ≥ 5 per cell. Fisher’s exact test counts tables directly and has no such requirement.

Q4. Complaints by weekday: Mon 42 … Sun 40, χ² = 4.0 on 6 df, p = 0.67. Your boss insists Mondays are worse. Best reply?

4.0 against a critical 12.6 is nowhere near. Selecting Monday after seeing it was highest and testing it alone is the multiple-comparisons trap in another costume.
