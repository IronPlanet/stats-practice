The t-Distribution

Born in the Guinness brewery, published under a fake name — the honest bell curve for small samples, and the referee behind every t-test you'll ever run.

1 · Where it came from — the best story in statistics

William Sealy Gosset worked as a chemist at the Guinness brewery in Dublin around 1908. Testing batches of beer meant tiny samples — you can’t run 10,000 trials on a brew. The normal-based methods of the day quietly lied when n was small: they were too confident.

Gosset worked out the correction. But Guinness forbade employees from publishing (trade-secret paranoia), so he published under the pseudonym “Student.” Every t-test ever run — in every lab, bank, and A/B platform on earth — is a tool born inside a beer factory, published by a man who couldn’t use his own name.

2 · The mathematics

t  =  x̄ − μs / √n     with   df = n − 1
x̄ — the sample mean · s — the sample’s own standard deviation (the culprit: it’s an estimate, adding uncertainty) · n — sample size · df — degrees of freedom, the dial that sets how heavy the tails are

Plain English: the t looks like the normal but with fatter tails — extra probability of extreme values, which is the honest price of estimating σ from a small sample. As df grows, the tails thin and the t melts into the normal (by n ≈ 30 they’re nearly twins).

Signature: same bell, heavier tails, one parameter (df). At df = 11 the 95% cutoff is 2.201, not the normal’s 1.96 — small samples must claim less.
When it applies: inference about MEANS when n is small (< ~30) and σ is unknown — which is almost always. It is not a model of raw data; it’s the referee for sample means.

3 · What it looks like — drag the degrees of freedom

area ABOVE t (shaded):  =T.DIST.RT(2, 11)  → 0.0354
Grey dashed curve = the normal. At low df the blue t-curve sits fatter in the tails; drag df up and watch it converge onto the normal.

The punchline to show the class: using the normal’s 1.96 instead of 2.201 gives a margin of \$3.45 — an interval that claims 95% confidence but doesn’t deliver it. The t’s wider \$3.88 is the honest version. Small samples pay a premium; the t is the price list.
Function map: T.INV.2T(α, df) = two-tail multiplier · T.DIST.RT(t, df) = right-tail p-value · T.TEST(range1, range2, tails, type) = the whole test in one cell (Week 3’s workhorse).

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. You have 12 stores' results for a pilot program. Why does the analysis use the t-distribution instead of the normal?

With little data you estimate the spread from the same small sample you're testing — double uncertainty. The t's wider tails are the honest surcharge

Q2. Degrees of freedom, in plain terms, tracks:

df ≈ information count. At df 5 the t is visibly fat-tailed; by df 30+ it's nearly the normal — which is where the '~30 rule of thumb' comes from

Q3. Same data, same confidence level: how does a t-based CI compare to a z-based one?

Fatter tails → bigger multiplier → wider interval. The width isn't a flaw; it's the honesty small data owes you
