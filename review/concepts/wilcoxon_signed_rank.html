The Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test

Wilcoxon’s 1945 idea for paired data — rank the differences by size, see which sign owns the big ranks, and count sign patterns instead of trusting a bell.

1 · Where it came from

1945, Stamford, Connecticut. Frank Wilcoxon is a 53-year-old chemist at American Cyanamid running assays on insecticides. His batches are small — six, eight, ten replicates — and the numbers are ugly: a skewed spread, an occasional wild reading. Student’s t-test, the only tool anyone used, assumes a bell that his data never showed. Wilcoxon’s four-page paper in Biometrics Bulletin (“Individual Comparisons by Ranking Methods”) proposed something radical: throw the numbers away and keep only their order. Two tests came out of it — the rank-sum test for two separate groups (today’s Mann–Whitney) and the signed-rank test for paired measurements: the same batch before and after, the same plot with two treatments, the same store in two months.

The signed-rank idea: for each pair, compute the difference; rank the differences by size, ignoring sign; then ask whether the big differences lean one way. If the treatment does nothing, plus and minus signs should be sprinkled randomly over the ranks and the two sign-sums should be about equal. Wilcoxon worked out the exact chances by counting sign patterns — no bell required. The test needed no table of the data’s distribution because it never used the data’s distribution. That is what “non-parametric” means.

2 · The mathematics

The question it answers. Same units measured twice. Is the typical change different from zero? It replaces the paired t-test when the differences are skewed, have outliers, or are few.
Step 1   di = afteri − beforei   (drop any pair with di = 0; n = pairs left)
Step 2   rank |d1| … |dn| from 1 (smallest) to n (largest); ties share the average rank
Step 3   W+ = Σ ranks where di > 0     W− = Σ ranks where di < 0     (check: W+ + W− = n(n+1)/2)
Step 4   W = min(W+, W−)  →  p-value from the exact sign-pattern count (small n) or the Normal approximation (n ≳ 15)
H0: the differences are symmetrically distributed around zero (equivalently, the median difference is 0) · H1: they are shifted (two-sided) · W — the smaller sign-sum; a small W means one sign has claimed the big ranks · Textbooks and software disagree on which number to print (W+, W−, or the min) — the p-value is the same; say which you report.

Where the p-value comes from — counting, not a table

Under H0 every difference is as likely to be positive as negative, independently. So each of the n ranks gets a + or a − by a fair coin: 2n equally likely sign patterns. For each pattern compute W+. The p-value is the fraction of patterns whose W is at least as extreme as yours (both tails). With n = 10 there are 1,024 patterns; a computer lists them in a millisecond, and you can do n = 5 by hand.

The floor. The most extreme pattern (all + or all −) occurs twice in 2n, so the smallest two-sided p is 2 / 2n: n = 5 → 0.0625 (can never reach 0.05) · n = 6 → 0.031 · n = 8 → 0.0078 · n = 10 → 0.002. Plan the number of pairs with this in mind.

Large samples — the Normal approximation

μW = n(n+1)4     σW = √n(n+1)(2n+1)24     z = W+ − μWσW     p = 2 · [1 − Φ(|z|)]
Good from about n = 15; software adds a continuity correction (±0.5) and a tie correction to σW. This z is also the route to the effect size.

The exact answer for these ten pairs — counting all 1,024 sign patterns with the tied ranks — is p = 0.0078 (the lab in §3 reproduces it). Software prints 0.008–0.012 depending on how it treats the two ties and whether it applies a continuity correction (scipy.stats.wilcoxon(after, before) gives 0.0098); every version says “about one in a hundred”. The Normal approximation’s 0.0125 is what Excel can do; say “approximate” when n < 15. Excel has no built-in Wilcoxon function; the recipe above is the test.
Business read: W⁺ = 3 means the only two increases were the two smallest changes (ranks 1.5 each). Eight of ten stores fell, typically by $4 a basket (r = 0.79, a large effect). “The layout change lowered the typical basket; roll it back” — and the paired t (p = 0.003) happens to agree here, but it was not entitled to an opinion on skewed differences.

6 · When to reach for it — and when not

Wilcoxon signed-rank when…Something else when…
Same units measured twice (before/after, two conditions on each subject) — a paired designTwo separate groups → Mann–Whitney U; three+ conditions on the same units → Friedman
Differences skewed, heavy-tailed, or n small (5–30)Differences roughly Normal or n ≳ 30 → paired t keeps the units and ~5 % more power
You need a verdict that one wild pair can’t hijackYou need “how much, with a CI, in dollars” — report the median difference / Hodges–Lehmann alongside, or bootstrap
Ordinal paired scores (satisfaction 1–5 before/after)Only signs are trustworthy (differences not comparable in size) → sign test
Three mistakes. (1) Ranking the raw values instead of the |differences| — that is a different test. (2) Describing the result as “means differ”; it is a rank/median statement. (3) Running it on 5 pairs and reporting “no effect” — the floor was 0.0625; the test could not have said yes.
Reporting line. “Wilcoxon signed-rank, n = 10 pairs, W⁺ = 3, exact p ≈ 0.01 (two-sided); median change −$4 per basket; r = 0.79.” Then one sentence on what the test cannot say (whether the drop persists, whether traffic changed).

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. Eight stores, before/after. Differences: −6, −3, +1, −9, −4, −2, +2, −7. Ranks of |d|: 1→1, 2→2.5, 2→2.5, 3→4, 4→5, 6→6, 7→7, 9→8. W⁺ = ?

Only positive differences contribute to W⁺: rank(|+1|) = 1 and rank(|+2|) = 2.5 (tied with −2). W⁻ = 36 − 3.5 = 32.5. Small W⁺ = the increases were the tiniest changes.

Q2. A manager runs the paired t and Wilcoxon on the same 12 pairs: t gives p = 0.21, Wilcoxon p = 0.02. The differences show one pair at +85, the rest between −6 and −1. Which do you report and why?

The +85 pair gets rank 12 whether it is +85 or +8,500. The t-test’s SE is driven by that one number. Report Wilcoxon, show the outlier separately, and say what happened at that store.

Q3. With n = 5 pairs every difference is negative: W⁺ = 0. Two-sided exact p?

All-minus is one of 32 patterns; the mirror image (all-plus) is another. 2/32. Five pairs cannot reject at α = 0.05 — design for six or more.

Q4. Which statement about the test’s H₀ is correct?

The test works on signed ranks of differences. It says nothing about means, and it assumes no distribution — only symmetry of the differences and independent pairs.
