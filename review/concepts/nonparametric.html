Non-Parametric Methods

Wilcoxon’s ranks, Pearson’s counts, Kolmogorov’s biggest gap, Kendall’s agreement — tests that work when the data refuse to be a bell.

1 · Where it came from

1945. Frank Wilcoxon is a chemist at American Cyanamid, not a statistician, and he is tired of t-tests on small, ugly batches of assay data. In a four-page paper he throws away the numbers, keeps only their order, and invents two tests at once: the rank-sum test for two independent groups and the signed-rank test for paired data. Two years later Henry Mann and his student Donald Whitney at Ohio State rederive the first one by counting pairs and give it the name U. William Kruskal & Allen Wallis (1952) push the same rank idea to three or more groups. None of these tests care what shape the data have — that is the whole point.

The older branch is about counts and shapes. Karl Pearson’s χ² (1900) asks whether a table of counts is far from “no association”. Andrey Kolmogorov (1933) and Nikolai Smirnov (1939) ask whether a whole distribution matches a reference — the biggest gap between two cumulative curves. Maurice Kendall (1939) measured how much a panel of judges agrees. Together they form the “distribution-free” toolkit: methods that work on ranks, categories, small samples and long tails — the data business actually produces.

2 · The mathematics — one idea, six tests

The idea: replace each value by its rank (its position in the sorted pile), or by a count in a bucket. A wild value can move one rank to the end of the line and no further, so no single observation can hijack the answer. The price: the test says which group tends to be bigger, not by how much — so always add an effect size in the data’s units.

Two independent groups — Mann–Whitney U (= Wilcoxon rank-sum)

U1  =  R1 − n1(n1+1)2      U2 = n1n2 − U1      H0: P(X < Y) = ½
R1 — sum of group 1’s ranks in the combined pile · U1 — the number of (x, y) pairs where x is the smaller · exact p = count the arrangements of ranks at least as lopsided (small n) or z = (U − n1n2/2) / √(n1n2(N+1)/12) (n ≳ 10 each) · effect sizes: Cliff’s δ = 2U/(n1n2) − 1 (−1…+1), r = z/√N, Hodges–Lehmann shift = median of all pairwise differences (in the data’s units). Full derivation, exact counting and an outlier drag-test: the deep dive.

Paired data — Wilcoxon signed-rank

di = afteri − beforei  →  drop d = 0  →  rank |di|  →  W+ = Σ ranks of positive d,   W− = Σ ranks of negative d  →  W = min(W+, W−)
Replaces the paired t. H0: the differences are symmetric about zero (median difference = 0). Exact p: enumerate the 2n ways of assigning signs to the ranks — so the smallest possible two-sided p is 2/2n (n = 5 can never reach 0.05; n = 6 bottoms out at 0.031). Large n: z = (W − n(n+1)/4) / √(n(n+1)(2n+1)/24). Effect size r = |z| / √n (0.1 small · 0.3 medium · 0.5 large), plus the median difference. Assumes the differences are roughly symmetric — a histogram of d checks it.

Three or more groups — Kruskal–Wallis H

H  =  12N(N+1) Σgroups ng R̄g2  −  3(N+1)      referee: χ²(k − 1)
Replaces one-way ANOVA. R̄g — mean rank in group g. If every group had the same typical rank (N+1)/2, H = 0. Follow a significant H with pairwise Mann–Whitney tests (Bonferroni-corrected) or Dunn’s test to find which groups differ. Effect size ε² = H / (N − 1) (or η²H = (H − k + 1)/(N − k)).

Counts in a table — χ² test of independence, with Cramér’s V

χ² = Σ (O − E)2E    E = row total × column totalgrand total    df = (r − 1)(c − 1)    V = √χ² / nmin(r − 1, c − 1)
H0: the two categorical variables are independent. Every expected count should be ≥ 5 (otherwise Fisher’s exact test). χ² tells you association exists, not its direction or size — Cramér’s V (0 … 1; ≈ 0.1 small · 0.3 medium · 0.5 large) does that. For a 2 × 2 table V equals |φ|. Live calculator and the goodness-of-fit version: field guide §5.

Does the whole shape match? — Kolmogorov–Smirnov D

D  =  maxx | Fsample(x) − Freference(x) |
The biggest vertical gap between the sample’s cumulative curve (the staircase) and the reference’s (a Normal with the sample’s mean and SD, or a second sample). p from Kolmogorov’s distribution: for large n, reject at 0.05 when D > 1.36/√n. Used as a normality test (one-sample KS; the Lilliefors variant when mean and SD are estimated) and as a two-sample test of distribution equality (sensitive to shape and spread, not just location). Less powerful than Shapiro–Wilk for normality; more general.

Do the judges agree? — Kendall’s W (coefficient of concordance)

W  =  12 Sm2(n3 − n)    S = Σitems (Ri − R̄)2      χ² = m(n − 1)W,  df = n − 1
m raters each rank n items; Ri = total rank of item i across raters. W = 0 no agreement, W = 1 perfect agreement. Read the value first: < 0.3 weak · 0.3–0.5 moderate · 0.5–0.7 strong · > 0.7 very strong. With many items even W = 0.2 is “significant” — significance says the agreement isn’t zero, W says whether it is enough to act on. Not to be confused with Kendall’s τ (a pairwise rank correlation). Spearman’s ρ is the two-rater special case, and the rank twin of Pearson’s r.

9 · When to reach for it — and when not

Go non-parametric when…Stay parametric when…
Small n and a skewed histogram or a Q–Q bow — the t-test’s borrowed bell is a lie heren ≳ 30 per group and no extreme outliers — the CLT makes the mean Normal and the t-test keeps its units
A few outliers would otherwise decide the verdict (one bad day, one whale customer)The business answer needs “how much” in dollars or seconds with a CI — ranks throw that away (report Hodges–Lehmann or the median difference alongside)
The data are ordinal — ratings 1–5, rankings, satisfaction scalesThe data are genuinely interval and roughly symmetric — the rank test loses ~5 % power for nothing
You have counts in categories (χ²) or whole distributions to compare (KS)You want to model several factors at once — ANOVA/regression have no clean rank equivalent
Non-parametric ≠ assumption-free. χ² needs expected counts ≥ 5. Wilcoxon signed-rank needs symmetric differences. Kendall’s W needs complete rankings. All of them need independent observations — 700 readings from 12 machines is 12 independent things. And a Wilcoxon or Mann–Whitney result is about ranks and medians: never describe it as a test of means.
Reporting line. “Mann–Whitney U = 0, n = 6 vs 12, exact p = 0.0001; Cliff’s δ = −1.0; Hodges–Lehmann shift −6.1 s.” Test · n · p · a bounded effect size · the shift in real units · one sentence on what the test cannot say.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. 49 product categories, average daily sales before and during a promotion. The paired differences are strongly skewed. Which test, and what does it test?

Same categories measured twice = paired. Non-Normal differences = the rank twin of the paired t. Mann–Whitney would throw away the pairing; a χ² needs counts, not sales figures.

Q2. A 4 × 2 table of class level by a two-level category gives χ² = 9.1, df = 3, p = 0.028, n = 800. What else must the report say?

χ² says an association exists; with n = 800 even a trivial one is significant. V ≈ 0.11 is small. Also check every expected count ≥ 5.

Q3. Five interviewers rank ten candidates. Kendall’s W = 0.22, χ² = 9.9, df = 9, p = 0.36. The HR director asks whether to extend the offer to the top-ranked candidate.

W = 0.22 is weak by convention and the test can’t even reject zero agreement. The interviewers evaluated different things; the “winner” is noise.

Q4. With 5 pairs and every difference positive, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test gives p = 0.0625. The right conclusion is:

Exact rank tests have a p-value floor. Five perfectly consistent pairs are the strongest possible evidence at that n and still miss 0.05. Choosing one-tailed after the fact is the oldest trick in the book.
