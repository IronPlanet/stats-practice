The Referees: χ² and F

Pearson's and Fisher's judging distributions — they never model your data; they rule on whether your test score could be luck.

Two distributions that never model your data

Everything else in this series describes the world: weights, counts, waits. These two do a different job — they are referees. You never say “my revenue is chi-square-distributed.” Instead, a TEST computes a score from your data, and the referee distribution says how surprising that score would be if nothing real were going on. Big score, tiny tail area → the “nothing real” story collapses.

Karl Pearson built the chi-square referee in 1900 — arguably the first modern hypothesis test — to judge whether observed category counts fit an expected pattern. Ronald Fisher built the F (named for him by George Snedecor) to judge variance ratios; it sits inside every ANOVA your Week-3 Thursday runs.

χ² · The category referee

χ²  =  Σ  (O − E)²E
O — observed count in each category · E — expected count if the null story were true · sum over all categories · df = categories − 1 (goodness-of-fit) or (rows−1)(cols−1) for tables

Plain English: how far do the counts sit from what boredom predicts, scaled so every category counts fairly? Zero means perfect agreement; the referee says how big is suspicious.

Where you meet it: goodness-of-fit (“are sales even across weekdays?”) and association in tables (“does membership tier relate to churn?”) — Week 3 Wednesday’s non-parametric toolkit.

F · The variance-ratio referee

F  =  variance BETWEEN groupsvariance WITHIN groups
Two df values: df₁ = groups − 1 (numerator) · df₂ = total − groups (denominator) · F ≈ 1 means the groups differ about as much as chance predicts; F ≫ 1 means the group gaps are too big to be luck
Where you meet it: printed in every ANOVA output (Week 3 Thursday). When Excel’s ANOVA table shows F = 4.85 with a p-value, that p IS the right-tail area of this curve.

What they look like — drag the degrees of freedom

right-tail p-value:  =CHISQ.DIST.RT(6.8, 3)  → 0.0786
right-tail p-value:  =F.DIST.RT(4.85, 2, 27)  → 0.0159
Both referees are right-skewed and only the RIGHT tail ever matters — a test score can only be suspiciously big, never suspiciously small.

Excel — run the χ² referee in class

Scenario: A store logged sales across four weekdays: Mon 55, Tue 45, Wed 38, Thu 62 (200 total). If day didn’t matter, each expects 50. Is the spread real?

The verdict discussion: p = 0.08. At α = 0.05 the referee says “not convicted” — the weekday spread could plausibly be luck. This is the perfect class argument-starter: Wednesday LOOKS terrible, and the test still refuses to convict. Eyeballs convict on vibes; referees demand evidence.
The F in the wild: you rarely type F formulas by hand — Excel’s Data ▸ Data Analysis ▸ ANOVA: Single Factor prints F, the df pair, and the p-value. =F.DIST.RT(4.85,2,27) = 0.0159 reproduces that p — three store formats, 30 stores, and the format differences are real at α = 0.05.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. Do product preferences (A/B/C) differ by region (North/South/East)? Both variables are categories. The referee is:

Category vs category = chi-square, comparing the observed contingency table to what independence would predict. t and correlation both want numeric variables

Q2. Your sales mix ran 50/30/20 across three lines historically; this quarter shows 44/31/25. Which question does chi-square GOODNESS-OF-FIT answer?

Goodness-of-fit compares observed category counts against an expected pattern. Profit ranking and forecasting are entirely different tools

Q3. HOW does chi-square actually judge a table of counts?

The statistic is an accumulated observed-vs-expected discrepancy; the chi-square distribution then says how much discrepancy pure chance produces. No means, no r — just counts
