The Kolmogorov–Smirnov Test

Kolmogorov’s 1933 universal curve — how far can a sample’s staircase wander from the truth by chance? One number, D, for whether a whole distribution fits.

1 · Where it came from

1933, Moscow. Andrey Kolmogorov — the man who had just put probability on rigorous axiomatic footing — asks a deceptively simple question: if I draw a sample from some distribution and plot the sample’s cumulative curve (the fraction of values at or below each point: a staircase that climbs from 0 to 1), how far can that staircase wander from the true cumulative curve just by chance? He proved the answer does not depend on what the distribution is — the largest vertical gap, scaled by √n, follows one universal curve. That gap is D, and the universal curve is what turns it into a p-value. Six years later Nikolai Smirnov extended it to two samples: the largest gap between two staircases tells you whether they came from the same distribution.

The test compares whole shapes, not just centres. A t-test asks “is the mean different?”; Mann–Whitney asks “does one group tend to be bigger?”; Kolmogorov–Smirnov asks “is anything about the distribution different — location, spread, skew, tails?” That generality is its strength and its weakness: it detects everything and is therefore not the most powerful test for any one thing. One footnote matters in practice: when you test against a Normal whose mean and SD you estimated from the same sample, Kolmogorov’s universal curve is too lenient; Hubert Lilliefors (1967) computed the corrected critical values. Software that says “KS test for normality” usually means Lilliefors.

2 · The mathematics

The question it answers. Does this sample look like it came from a given distribution (one-sample)? Do these two samples come from the same distribution (two-sample)? Any difference counts.
Fn(x) = number of observations ≤ xn      D = maxx | Fn(x) − F(x) |
Fn — the empirical cumulative distribution (ECDF): the staircase; each observation adds a step of height 1/n · F — the reference cumulative curve (a Normal with some μ, σ; an Exponential; …) or, for two samples, the other staircase · D — the tallest vertical gap anywhere. At each sorted observation x(i) check both the top of the step, i/n − F(x(i)), and the bottom, F(x(i)) − (i−1)/n; D is the largest of all of them.

The referee — Kolmogorov’s distribution

P(√n · D ≤ λ) → 1 − 2 Σk=1∞ (−1)k−1 e−2k²λ²      critical D at α = 0.05 ≈ 1.36√n  (α = 0.01: 1.63/√n)   for n ≳ 35; exact tables below that
Notice what is not in the formula: the shape of F. That is the “distribution-free” property. Two-sample version: D = max |Fn(x) − Gm(x)| with effective n = nm/(n+m) in the same formula.

The Lilliefors correction

If μ and σ were estimated from the data, the fitted Normal has been pulled toward the sample and the gap D is systematically smaller than Kolmogorov assumed. Using his critical value then fails to reject too often. Lilliefors’ critical D at α = 0.05 ≈ 0.886/√n (vs 1.36/√n) — about 35 % tighter. Use it whenever the reference parameters came from the sample. statsmodels.stats.diagnostic.lilliefors does this; plain scipy.stats.kstest does not.

Read: the fitted Normal is furthest from the data at 6.1 days — the staircase has already climbed to 0.75 while the bell says 0.53. Because μ and σ came from the sample, Lilliefors applies: D = 0.216 > 0.198, not Normal. (Shapiro–Wilk agrees: W = 0.83, p = 0.002.) Plain KS would have said p ≈ 0.27 — the classic false comfort.
Business read: a Normal capacity model would put the 95th percentile at 5.8 + 1.645 × 3.4 ≈ 11.4 days; the data’s own 95th percentile is ≈ 12.6 and the tail runs to 15. Plan on the empirical percentiles or a log-Normal, not the bell.

Reference distribution fully specifiedDid μ, σ come from the same sample?Yes → Lilliefors critical values (or a bootstrap); the plain table is too lenient
Two-sample: same conditions, no pairingDesignPaired → Wilcoxon signed-rank on the differences
What D is sensitive to. The cumulative curves are steepest in the middle, so a shift in the centre opens a big gap fast; the tails are flat, so a heavy tail barely moves D. If tails are what you care about (risk, extremes), Anderson–Darling is the sharper tool.

6 · When to reach for it — and when not

Kolmogorov–Smirnov when…Something else when…
You want to know whether two samples differ in any way — spread and shape, not just centre (two-sample KS)The question is “which tends to be bigger?” → Mann–Whitney is more powerful for that
You must test fit to a non-Normal reference (Exponential arrivals, a log-Normal, last year’s empirical distribution)Normality specifically, n < 50 → Shapiro–Wilk
A visual, explainable check — the staircase plot is the argumentCategorical or heavily tied data → χ² goodness of fit
Parameters known in advance (a spec, a supplier’s claim)Parameters estimated from the sample → Lilliefors, not the plain table
Three mistakes. (1) Using the plain KS table after fitting μ and σ from the data — it fails to reject far too often. (2) “KS p = 0.3, so the data are Normal” — failing to reject is not confirming; with n = 20 the test has little power. (3) Running KS on 1,000 rows and switching methods because p = 0.02 — at that n the departure may be trivial; look at D itself (0.03 is nothing) and the plot.
Reporting line. “Lilliefors-corrected KS, n = 20, D = 0.216 (critical 0.198), Normal rejected; skew 1.59; empirical 95th percentile 12.6 days.” Then what it cannot say: which alternative distribution is right — KS rejects, it doesn’t recommend.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. You fit a Normal to 30 delivery times using their own mean and SD, get D = 0.20, and look up Kolmogorov’s critical value 1.36/√30 = 0.248. Conclusion?

The plain table assumes the reference was specified in advance. Fitting μ and σ from the sample pulls the curve toward the staircase and shrinks D; the corrected threshold is a third tighter.

Q2. Two suppliers’ lead times have the same median but supplier B’s vary twice as much. Which test detects that?

Mann–Whitney tests whether one group tends to be bigger; with equal centres it sees nothing. KS’s D responds to any difference in the cumulative curves.

Q3. D is computed as the largest gap between cumulative curves. Where is the test least sensitive?

The ECDF climbs fastest near the centre, so centre shifts open gaps quickly. Tail behaviour (risk, extremes) is better caught by Anderson–Darling.

Q4. n = 15, KS p = 0.41. The analyst writes “the data are confirmed Normal.” Problem?

Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. Small samples rarely reject anything; the picture and the business stakes decide.
