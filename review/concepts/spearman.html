Spearman’s ρ — Rank Correlation

Spearman’s 1904 schoolmaster’s trick — rank both variables, then correlate the ranks. Do they move together, whatever the curve, whoever the whale?

1 · Where it came from

1904. Charles Spearman, a former British army officer who took up psychology at 45, is trying to show that children who do well at one school subject tend to do well at others — the argument that became his theory of general intelligence, g. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (1895) existed, but Spearman’s data were teachers’ orderings of pupils, not measurements, and he wanted something a schoolmaster could compute by hand. His paper in the American Journal of Psychology gave the trick: rank each variable, then correlate the ranks. The formula with the Σd² that every textbook prints is just Pearson’s r applied to ranks, simplified for the case with no ties.

What Spearman bought with that trick is robustness and generality. Pearson’s r measures how well a straight line fits, and one whale of a point can drag the line anywhere. Spearman’s ρ measures whether the relationship is monotone — as one goes up, does the other tend to go up — whatever the curve’s shape, and a whale is just the highest rank. It is the rank-based twin of Pearson’s r, exactly as Mann–Whitney is the twin of the t-test.

2 · The mathematics

The question it answers. Two variables measured on the same units. When one is higher, does the other tend to be higher (or lower)? Strength and direction of a monotone relationship, on a −1 … +1 scale, immune to outliers and to the shape of the curve.
Step 1   rank x1 … xn → Rx; rank y1 … yn → Ry (ties share the average rank)
Step 2   di = Rx,i − Ry,i for each unit
Step 3   ρ = 1 − 6 Σ di2n(n2 − 1)   (no ties)     — in general: ρ = Pearson’s r computed on the two rank columns
Step 4   significance: t = ρ √n − 21 − ρ2 on df = n − 2   (n ≳ 10; exact tables below)
H0: no monotone association (ρ = 0) · ρ = +1: the orderings agree perfectly, whatever the curve · ρ = −1: perfectly reversed · Σd²: 0 when the ranks match; its maximum n(n² − 1)/3 when reversed — the 6/(n(n²−1)) scales it to −1 … +1 · ρ² is not “variance explained” in the Pearson sense; read ρ itself: 0.1 weak · 0.3 moderate · 0.5 strong (same conventions as r).

Kendall’s τ is the other rank correlation: the share of concordant pairs minus discordant pairs. Same sign as ρ, usually smaller in magnitude, better with many ties and tiny n. Spearman is the default; τ is the alternative — and neither is Kendall’s W, the multi-rater concordance.

6 · When to reach for it — and when not

Spearman ρ when…Something else when…
Outliers or skew would hijack Pearson (revenue, spend, tenure)Roughly linear, bell-shaped, no whales → Pearson r gives a slope and a CI in units
Ordinal data: satisfaction scores, rankings, Likert scalesYou want to predict y from x → regression
The relationship is curved but monotone (diminishing returns)Non-monotone (rises then falls) → neither coefficient; model the curve
Checking a dashboard’s “these move together” claim quickly and robustlySeveral raters’ rankings → Kendall’s W
Three mistakes. (1) Using the Σd² formula with many ties — it is only exact without ties; correlate the average ranks instead. (2) Reading ρ = 0.9 as “90 % explained” or as causation. (3) Reporting a significant ρ of 0.12 from n = 2,000 as a finding — p rewards n; ρ says the link is weak.
Reporting line. “Spearman ρ = 0.95, n = 10, p < 0.001 (Pearson r = 0.76, pulled down by one large account).” Then what it cannot say: direction of cause, and the slope in dollars.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. Ten accounts: Pearson r = 0.75, Spearman ρ = 0.95. One account spends four times more than any other. What happened?

r measures a line’s fit and one far-out point dominates it. ρ measures order. Both are “right” about different questions; the business question (do they move together?) is ρ’s.

Q2. y = x³ exactly for x = 1 … 10. Pearson r and Spearman ρ?

ρ asks “monotone?” — yes, perfectly. r asks “linear?” — no, so it falls short of 1.

Q3. Satisfaction (1–5) vs tenure (years) for 2,000 customers: ρ = 0.09, p = 0.00006. The dashboard headline says “tenure drives satisfaction”. Objections?

Significance scales with n; effect size does not. And ρ is symmetric — it cannot tell tenure→satisfaction from satisfaction→tenure or a third cause.

Q4. Twelve stores rated 1–5 on cleanliness and 1–5 on service. Many ties. Which computation?

With average ranks the shortcut formula drifts; the general definition (Pearson on ranks) handles ties correctly. τ-b is the tie-robust alternative.
