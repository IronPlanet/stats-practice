The Binomial Distribution

Bernoulli's answer to every 'how many out of n?' question — closes out of calls, defects out of units, no-shows out of bookings.

1 · Where it came from

Jacob Bernoulli (1654–1705) belonged to a Swiss dynasty of mathematicians so prolific they needed first names to tell them apart. He formalized the Bernoulli trial: any situation with exactly two outcomes — heads/tails, sale/no-sale, defect/no-defect. His question: repeat a yes/no experiment n times — what’s the probability of exactly k successes?

His masterwork Ars Conjectandi appeared in 1713, eight years after his death, and the answer inside it is the binomial distribution. Three hundred years later it prices every “how many out of n?” question a business asks: closes out of 20 calls, defects out of 500 units, no-shows out of 40 reservations.

2 · The mathematics

P(X = k)  =  n!k! (n−k)!  ·  pk (1−p)n−k
n — the number of trials (fixed in advance) · k — the number of successes you’re asking about · p — the probability of success on each trial (constant) · the fraction is “n choose k” — how many orderings give exactly k successes

Plain English: count the ways to place k successes among n trials, times the probability of any one such arrangement.

Mean = np   ·   Variance = np(1−p)
The four-item checklist (all must hold): fixed number of trials n · each trial yes/no · same p every trial · trials independent. Break one — the binomial breaks. A rep who improves during the day breaks “same p.” Word-of-mouth referrals break independence.

3 · What it looks like — drag n and p

P(X ≥ k) — orange bars:  =1-BINOM.DIST(8, 20, 0.3, TRUE)  → 0.1133
Note the formula uses k−1: “9 or more” = 1 minus “8 or fewer.” Push p to 0.5 and watch the symmetry; grow n and watch the bell emerge.
The three question patterns: Exactly k = BINOM.DIST(k, n, p, FALSE) · At most k = BINOM.DIST(k, n, p, TRUE) · At least k = 1 - BINOM.DIST(k−1, n, p, TRUE) — mind the k−1.

The k−1 trap: “at least 9” means the 9 bar counts, so you subtract the cumulative up to 8. Writing 1-BINOM.DIST(9,…,TRUE) silently throws the 9 bar away — the most common exam mistake in this topic.
Business read: a 30% closer has a bonus day about once every 9 working days. If the bonus is \$100, that’s ≈ \$11/day of expected bonus cost — price the incentive plan accordingly.

5 · Does real data fit?

How to check fit: the sample mean should sit near np, and the spread near np(1−p). Then overlay fitted bars. If real variance is much bigger than np(1−p), the “same p, independent trials” assumptions are cracking.

6 · When to reach for it — and when not

Reach for Binomial when…Do NOT use Binomial when…
k successes out of a FIXED n trialsThere’s no fixed n — you’re counting events per window (Poisson)
Each trial is yes/no with constant pp changes across trials (fatigue, learning, hot streaks)
Trials are independentOutcomes influence each other (referrals, contagion, shared causes)
n large & p small? λ = np and the Poisson does the same job with easier arithmetic.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. You send 200 quotes and each independently has about the same chance of being accepted. To model HOW MANY get accepted, you choose the binomial because:

The binomial signature is fixed n + yes/no + constant p + independence. Continuous data wants normal; events-over-time wants Poisson

Q2. Which situation BREAKS the binomial's assumptions?

Trials must be INDEPENDENT with the SAME p. If one outcome changes the next trial's chances (fatigue, contagion, learning), the binomial's math quietly stops describing your process

Q3. HOW does a manager actually use a binomial model of 500 emails with a 4% click rate?

The binomial answers count questions: expected successes and how unusual a given count is. Timing questions belong to exponential; trends to regression
