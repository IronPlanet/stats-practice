Sampling & Estimation

Why a sample mean has its own distribution, what the square-root penalty costs you, and how to read an interval without overclaiming.

1 · Where it came from

In 1908 a Guinness brewer named William Sealy Gosset had a problem no textbook covered: he could afford four or five samples of barley, not four hundred, and the normal curve gave him confidence intervals that were far too narrow at that size. Guinness forbade staff from publishing, so he signed his solution “Student”. The t-distribution is a brewer's answer to small samples, and it is still the right one.

The deeper idea is older and stranger. A sample mean is not a number — it is a draw from its own distribution. Take another sample and you get another mean. The Central Limit Theorem says that this distribution of means becomes bell-shaped as n grows no matter what shape the underlying data has. That is what licenses nearly every interval and test in this course.

2 · The mathematics

SE = s√n      x̄ ± tα/2, n−1 · SE
s — how spread out the individual observations are. SE — how spread out the sample mean is. These are different quantities and confusing them is the single most common error in this topic. More data does not make your customers more alike; it makes your estimate of their average steadier.
The √n penalty. SE falls with the square root of n, not with n. Halving your uncertainty costs four times the data. Cutting it to a tenth costs a hundred times. Every “can we just collect a bit more?” conversation is really about this curve.
Wilson interval for a proportion:   p̂ + z2/2n1 + z2/n  ±  z √( p̂(1−p̂)/n + z2/4n2 )1 + z2/n
The textbook interval p̂ ± z√(p̂(1−p̂)/n) is the one most people reach for and it is wrong near 0 and 1 — it can hand you a lower bound below zero, and its real coverage drifts well under 95%. Wilson fixes both and costs one extra term. Clopper–Pearson is the exact alternative, available in Excel through BETA.INV; it never undercovers but is conservative.

The misreading that costs marks. “n > 30 so the data is normal” is false. The CLT says nothing about your raw observations — they keep their skew forever. It describes the behaviour of the mean. A right-skewed column is still right-skewed at n = 5,000.

5 · Reading intervals like an analyst

Overlap is the fastest honest test you have. Plot group means with their intervals. If every interval overlaps every other one, the apparent ranking is noise and no action follows from it. Saying “Region C is our worst performer” when C's interval covers three other regions' means is the most common way a deck misleads a room.
What 95% actually means. It describes the procedure, not this one interval: over many repetitions, 95% of intervals built this way contain the true value. It is not a 95% probability that the parameter sits inside the interval you are holding — the parameter is fixed, and your interval either caught it or missed it.
One-sided or two? Decide from the claim, before you see the data. “Wait times are under five minutes” is a one-sided claim and deserves a one-sided bound. Quoting the lower end of a two-sided interval as if it were a one-sided bound overstates your confidence, and switching to one-sided after a two-sided test disappoints you is moving the goalposts.

6 · Assumptions, and when these break

ToolQuietly assumesBreaks when
SE = s/√nindependent observations, one populationclustered or repeated-measures data — the true SE is larger, sometimes far larger
t intervalroughly normal sampling distributionvery small n with heavy skew; at n < 15 with a long tail, prefer a rank method or a bootstrap
Wilson intervalindependent trials, fixed nthe same customer contributes several trials
Any intervalthe sample represents the population you care aboutconvenience samples — no arithmetic repairs a biased frame, and more data makes it worse, not better
Sample size fixes noise, never bias. A wobbly estimate from a fair sample converges. A precise estimate from a skewed frame converges on the wrong answer, with a narrower interval each time to prove it.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. A team reports that their 4,000-row dataset is “normal because n > 30”. What is wrong?

Raw observations keep their shape at any n. A right-skewed column is still right-skewed with four thousand rows. What becomes bell-shaped is the distribution of x̄ across repeated samples — which is what makes the t interval work, and says nothing about whether the data itself is normal.

Q2. An estimate has a margin of error of ±$4.00 at n = 50. Roughly what n gives ±$2.00?

SE falls with √n, so halving the margin needs four times the data: 50 × 4 = 200. This is why precision gets expensive fast, and why the honest answer to “can we tighten this a bit?” is usually a budget number.

Q3. Four regions' mean revenues are plotted with 95% intervals, and every interval overlaps every other. The correct conclusion is:

Fully overlapping intervals mean the data cannot distinguish the regions. Reporting a ranking anyway — even hedged with ‘not significantly’ — invites the room to act on an order the evidence does not support.

Q4. A survey of website visitors finds 62% satisfaction with a very tight interval. The sample only reached people who completed a purchase. What does the tight interval tell you?

The interval measures noise, not bias. A skewed sampling frame converges on the wrong answer, and a larger sample tightens the interval around that wrong answer. No arithmetic repairs a frame that excluded the people who left.
