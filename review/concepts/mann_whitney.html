The Mann–Whitney U test, from the ground up

What it actually tests, why ranks make it immune to a wild value, how the p-value is counted rather than looked up, and how to read the result — worked on six machines from a new supplier against twelve existing machines.

0Three names, one test

Mann–Whitney U, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, and the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test are the same procedure. Wilcoxon (1945) added up ranks; Mann and Whitney (1947) counted pairs; the two numbers differ by a constant you can compute in your head, and the p-value is identical. Software labels the statistic U or W depending on which paper the author read.

The test that is not the same is the Wilcoxon signed-rank test — that is for paired data (the same units measured twice). This page is about the two-independent-groups test only.

1The problem the test was built for

You have two groups of numbers and want to know whether one group tends to be bigger than the other — and you do not trust the average.

Six machines from a new supplier (group C) and twelve existing machines (group A) each ran for fourteen days. Summarise each machine by its typical day (its median response time, in seconds):

Group C: 17.3 · 17.5 · 17.9 · 19.2 · 19.4 · 19.5
Group A: 22.7 · 23.0 · 23.4 · 23.8 · 24.8 · 25.1 · 25.2 · 25.2 · 25.4 · 26.4 · 27.0 · 27.0

Now summarise each machine by its mean instead. Five of the C nodes barely move. The sixth, C5, had outages on two of its fourteen days and spent them answering in five to nine minutes; its mean is 78.8 s.

Group C (means): 18.4 · 20.0 · 17.6 · 20.3 · 78.8 · 18.1
Group A (means): 26.2 · 24.1 · 24.8 · 27.2 · 23.0 · 26.1 · 24.3 · 26.3 · 24.2 · 26.4 · 25.4 · 26.4

A t-test on the six means against the twelve says p = 0.74 — "no difference." One number out of eighteen decided that. The t-test is doing exactly what it is designed to do: it measures the gap between the two averages in units of the spread, and C5 has made group C's average larger than A's and its spread enormous. The question is whether that is the question you wanted answered.

The question Mann–Whitney answers instead: if I pick one group C machine at random and one group A machine at random, how often is the C machine faster? If the two groups are really the same, the answer is half the time. If C is really faster, it is more than half. That question does not care how much faster — only which one wins.

2The idea: replace values with ranks

Take all eighteen numbers, throw them into one pile, sort them, and number them 1 to 18 from smallest to largest. Those position numbers are the ranks. Then forget the original values entirely.

Why this helps. The largest value in the pile gets rank 18. It gets rank 18 if it is 27.2 and it gets rank 18 if it is 78.8 or 78,000. The rank records that it is the biggest, not how much bigger. So a single wild value can move one rank to the end of the line — and no further. That is the entire source of the test's robustness, and also of its blind spot: it cannot tell a gap of 1 second from a gap of 60.

Now the logic of the test in one sentence: if the two groups were interchangeable, the six group C ranks would be a random handful of six numbers drawn from 1…18. Sometimes that handful would be low, sometimes high, usually middling. We can write down every possible handful, see how unusual ours is, and that fraction is the p-value. No bell curve, no standard deviation, no assumption about the shape of the data — just counting.

3What U actually counts

The statistic has two equivalent readings. Understanding both is what makes the test stop feeling arbitrary.

Reading 1 — pairs. Line up every possible (C, A) pair. With 6 and 12 machines there are 6 × 12 = 72 pairs. UC is the number of pairs in which the C value is smaller. If the groups were identical you'd expect about half, 36. If every C is below every A, UC = 0. If every C is above every A, UC = 72.

Reading 2 — rank sums. Sum the ranks of the C group: RC. The smallest possible sum is 1+2+…+6 = 21 (all six C machines are the six smallest). UC = RC − 21. In general:

U1 = R1 − n1(n1+1)2     U2 = n1n2 − U1

The two readings agree exactly: subtracting the minimum possible rank sum converts "sum of positions" into "number of opponents beaten." That is the constant separating Wilcoxon's W from Mann and Whitney's U. Software reports the smaller of U1 and U2, or sometimes U1 for the first group named — check which, because the p-value is the same either way but the number is not.

4Where the p-value comes from: counting, not a table

Here is the step that is usually skipped and is the reason the test feels fuzzy. The p-value of a Mann–Whitney test is a count.

  1. Under H₀ ("the groups are interchangeable"), every way of assigning 6 of the 18 ranks to group C is equally likely. There are C(18, 6) = 18,564 such ways.
  2. For each of those 18,564 ways, compute U. Tally how many ways give each value of U. That tally is the null distribution of U — it is what "just luck" looks like.
  3. Find where the U you actually observed sits in that tally. The fraction of ways that give a U at least as extreme (both tails, for a two-sided test) is the p-value.

For our data UC = 0: all six C machines hold the six lowest ranks. Exactly one of the 18,564 arrangements does that, and one more gives the mirror image (all six highest). p = 2 / 18,564 = 0.000108. No distribution was assumed anywhere; we counted arrangements.

7What H₀ really says (and what it does not)

Most textbooks write H₀ as "the two medians are equal." That is a convenient shorthand and it is not what the test checks. The precise null hypothesis is:

H₀: P(X < Y) = ½   a random draw from group 1 is equally likely to fall below or above a random draw from group 2

The quantity P(X < Y) is called the probability of superiority, and U estimates it directly: UC / (n₁n₂) = 0 / 72 = 0 here, meaning a C machine beats an A machine 100 % of the time. If the two groups have the same shape and differ only by a shift, then "P(X < Y) = ½" and "medians equal" are the same statement. If the shapes differ — one skewed, one symmetric, one much wider — they can come apart: two groups can have identical medians and still have one "usually below" the other. So say "one group tends to be lower," and only say "medians differ" when the histograms look alike.

Assumptions the test does make: the observations are independent (18 machines, not 18 × 14 daily rows that share days), the data are at least ordinal (bigger/smaller is meaningful), and the two samples are drawn independently. Assumptions it does not make: Normality, equal variances, large n.

8How big is the difference? Effect sizes for U

A p-value of 0.0001 says the pattern is not luck. It says nothing about whether the gap matters. Three ways to say how big:

MeasureFormulaExample (18 medians)How to read it
Probability of superiorityUC / (n₁n₂)0 / 72 = 0.00 → C faster 100 % of pairs0.5 = coin flip; 0 or 1 = complete separation
Cliff's δ(pairs C > A − pairs C < A) / n₁n₂(0 − 72)/72 = −1.00like a correlation: |δ| ≈ 0.15 small · 0.33 medium · 0.47 large
Rank-biserial r1 − 2U / n₁n₂1 − 0 = 1.00same information as δ, opposite sign convention
Hodges–Lehmann shiftmedian of all n₁n₂ pairwise differences (C − A)−6.1 sthe number in the data's own units — what management wants

δ and r are unit-free and bounded; the Hodges–Lehmann estimate is the robust cousin of "difference in means." Report one bounded measure plus the shift in seconds.

9Larger samples, and ties

The normal approximation. When both groups have about ten or more observations, the null distribution of U (you saw it become a bell in §4) is close enough to Normal that you can skip the counting:

μU = n1n22   σU = √n1n2(n1+n2+1)12   z = U − μUσU

For 6 vs 12: μ = 36, σ = √(72 × 19 / 12) = 10.7, z = (0 − 36)/10.7 = −3.37, p ≈ 0.0008. The exact answer was 0.0001; the approximation is conservative here because n₁ = 6 is small. Software often adds a continuity correction (±0.5 to U).

Ties. Two equal values share the average of the ranks they would have taken: 25.2 and 25.2 at positions 13 and 14 both get 13.5. Ties make the exact count slightly inaccurate, so most software switches to the normal approximation (with a variance correction) as soon as any tie exists. That is why scipy.stats.mannwhitneyu reports p = 0.0009 on the rounded medians (two ties) and p = 0.0001 on the unrounded ones — same data, same U, different p-value route. Don't round before you rank.

10How to run it

Python (preferred)

from scipy import stats
C = [17.3, 17.5, 17.9, 19.2, 19.4, 19.5]
A = [22.7, 23.0, 23.4, 23.8, 24.8, 25.1,
     25.2, 25.2, 25.4, 26.4, 27.0, 27.0]
res = stats.mannwhitneyu(C, A, alternative="two-sided",
                         method="exact")   # "auto" switches on ties
res.statistic   # U for the first argument (C) = 0
res.pvalue
# effect sizes
import numpy as np
U = res.statistic; n1, n2 = len(C), len(A)
cliff = (2*U/(n1*n2)) - 1          # here −1.0  (sign: C below A)
hl = np.median([c - a for c in C for a in A])   # −6.1

Excel (no built-in test)

  1. Put all 18 values in one column with a group label beside them.
  2. =RANK.AVG(value, all_values, 1) — ascending, ties averaged.
  3. =SUMIF(group_col, "C", rank_col) → RC.
  4. UC = RC − n₁(n₁+1)/2.
  5. For the p-value use the z formula in §9 with =2*(1-NORM.S.DIST(ABS(z),TRUE)), or look U up in a Mann–Whitney table for small n. Excel cannot do the exact count.
Reporting line. "Mann–Whitney U = 0, n = 6 vs 12, exact two-sided p = 0.0001; Cliff's δ = −1.0 (complete separation); Hodges–Lehmann shift −6.1 s (C faster)." Then say, in a sentence, which question the test answered — typical machine, not the total over the fortnight — and put the outage days in their own paragraph.

11Self-check

1. Group X has ranks 2, 5, 9 out of 9 values (n₁ = 3, n₂ = 6). What is UX?

RX = 16; UX = 16 − 3·4/2 = 10. Check by pairs: rank 2 beats 1 Y value, rank 5 beats 3, rank 9 beats 6 — total 10 pairs where X is larger; so UX counts "X larger" here (1+3+6 = 10) and UY = 18 − 10 = 8. Either convention works; report which.

2. If the largest group C value jumped from 19.5 s to 1,000 s, what would happen to UC, and to the t-test?

That value moves from rank 6 to rank 18: UC goes from 0 to 12 and stays there no matter how large the value gets. The t-test's p-value keeps rising toward 1 as the value grows. Bounded vs unbounded influence.

3. Why is the smallest possible two-sided p for 6 vs 12 exactly 2/18,564 and not smaller?

Because only one arrangement of ranks gives U = 0 and only one gives U = 72; there is nothing more extreme to count. With small groups the exact p has a floor, just as the Wilcoxon signed-rank test does (2/2ⁿ). Plan the sample size with the floor in mind.

4. Two groups have the same median but one is much more spread out. Can Mann–Whitney reject?

Yes, if the spread is asymmetric enough that one group's values tend to fall below the other's. H₀ is P(X < Y) = ½, not "medians equal." Say "tends to be lower," and check the histograms before saying "median."

5. You have 756 rows (18 machines × 14 days × 3 time windows). Why not run Mann–Whitney on all of them?

Rows on the same day share that day's conditions; windows on the same machine-day share a machine. They are not independent draws, and the counting in §4 assumes they are — so the p-value would be far too small. Collapse to one number per independent unit (the machine) first.
