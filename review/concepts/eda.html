Exploratory Data Analysis

Tukey's disciplined first look — six questions, ten minutes, and most analytical disasters die before they're born.

1 · Where it came from

John Tukey — Princeton mathematician, coiner of the words “bit” and “software” — spent the 1960s watching analysts torture data with formal tests they chose before looking at anything. His 1977 book Exploratory Data Analysis flipped the order: look first, with humble tools — the boxplot and stem-and-leaf plot are his inventions — and let the data suggest the questions.

His line still runs the discipline: “The greatest value of a picture is when it forces us to notice what we never expected to see.” EDA is the disciplined first look — before modeling, before testing, before believing.

2 · The toolkit

Five-number summary:   min · Q1 · median · Q3 · max
Outlier fences:   Q1 − 1.5·IQR  and  Q3 + 1.5·IQR    where   IQR = Q3 − Q1
Q1, Q3 — the 25th and 75th percentiles · IQR — the middle 50%’s width · anything beyond a fence is flagged for investigation — never silently deleted
The checklist, in order: ① what does one ROW represent? ② column types right? ③ missing values — how many, and random or systematic? ④ impossible values (negative ages, future dates)? ⑤ duplicates? ⑥ five-number summary per numeric column. Six questions, ten minutes, and most disasters die here.
Ethics is an EDA job: a column that proxies a protected class (zip ↔ race), or one demographic’s data systematically missing, gets caught HERE or baked into every model downstream. “The data said so” is not a defense — someone chose what to collect and keep.

3 · The outlier experiment — drag one point

mean = 228.5  ·  median = 206.0  ·  upper fence = 230.5  ·  verdict: OUTLIER — investigate
Drag the slider: the mean chases the point, the median barely moves, and the fence tells you when to start asking questions. That asymmetry is half of EDA.

4 · Excel — run it in class

Scenario: 12 days of order counts in A1:A12 — 212, 198, 205, 220, 201, 195, 208, 214, 199, 203, 480, 207. Day 11 looks weird. Profile it:

celltype thismeaningyou should see
C1=AVERAGE(A1:A12)the mean — outlier-sensitive228.5
C2=MEDIAN(A1:A12)the middle — robust206
C3=QUARTILE.INC(A1:A12,1)Q1200.5
C4=QUARTILE.INC(A1:A12,3)Q3212.5
C5=C4-C3IQR12
C6=C4+1.5*C5upper outlier fence230.5
C7=COUNTIF(A1:A12,">"&C6)how many rows to investigate1
The 23-point gap between C1 and C2 IS the finding. Mean 228.5 vs median 206: one day is dragging the story. Was 480 a data-entry slip (48.0?), a promotion day, or a discovery? The spreadsheet can’t tell you — that’s the investigation EDA demands before anyone models “typical demand.”
More profiling artillery: =COUNTBLANK(range) for missingness · =MIN / =MAX for impossible values · =SUMPRODUCT(--(COUNTIF(A1:A12,A1:A12)>1)) to count duplicated values.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. Your merged customer file shows mean spend $228 but median spend $206. The FIRST professional move is:

A mean-median gap signals skew or outliers; the investigation (error? promotion? discovery?) comes before any reporting decision.

Q2. 30% of the 'region' column is blank, all from one source system. This missingness is:

Missingness tied to a source is systematic; silent ignoring or invented fills bias every regional conclusion.

Q3. Tukey's outlier fences flag a value at Q3 + 1.5·IQR. The fence's job is to:

Fences are tripwires, not executioners — flagged points get investigated (error, rare event, or discovery), never silently dropped.
