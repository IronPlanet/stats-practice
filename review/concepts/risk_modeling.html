Risk Modeling & Expected Value

Wald's bombers, von Neumann's games, Raiffa's tables — refusing to pretend there's only one future, and pricing the ones there are.

1 · Where it came from

WWII. The US military studied bullet holes on bombers returning from missions — dense clusters on fuselage, wings, tail — and planned to armor those spots. Abraham Wald, a Hungarian mathematician at Columbia’s Statistical Research Group, said the opposite: armor where there are NO holes — engines, cockpit, fuel. The planes hit there never came back to be counted. That’s survivorship bias: the data you can’t see is often more important than the data you can. (Every “here’s what successful companies did” article makes the military’s mistake.)

The same war produced the toolkit: von Neumann & Morgenstern’s 1944 Theory of Games and Economic Behavior gave decisions-under-uncertainty their mathematics, and Howard Raiffa carried it into business schools — payoff tables, decision trees, and the discipline of pricing futures you can’t predict.

2 · The mathematics

EMV(decision)  =  Σ  pj · Vj
pj — probability of state-of-the-world j (must sum to 1) · Vj — the payoff of this decision if state j happens · read it as: “if I made this call 1,000 times, what would I average?”
Three readings of one payoff table: EMV — weight by probabilities, pick the highest (the repeat-player’s rule) · Maximin — compare worst cases, protect the downside (the survival rule) · Maximax — compare best cases (the optimist’s rule). They disagree exactly when the decision is genuinely hard — and the disagreement is information.
EVPI — the Expected Value of Perfect Information: what a crystal ball would be worth. It caps what you should ever pay for research, forecasts, or consultants.

A repeat-player chain takes the 34; a founder whose company dies at −20 takes the 23.5. The analyst presents EMV AND the spread AND the worst case; the decision-maker chooses the appetite.
EVPI read: a perfect forecast is worth 40 − 34 = $6k. Any consultant charging more than $6k for market certainty is charging more than certainty is worth.

5 · When to reach for it — and when not

Model the risk when…Hold off when…
Outcomes and rough probabilities can be listed (repeatable decisions: pricing, stocking, insuring) Probabilities are pure invention — garbage p’s make confident garbage EMVs
The decision recurs — long-run averaging appliesOne-shot, bet-the-company calls — survival beats averages; weight maximin
You’ll show sensitivity (flip points) alongside the answerThe table is being used to END an argument instead of INFORM it

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. Option A: EMV $34k, worst case −$20k. Option B: EMV $23.5k, worst case +$10k. A cash-strapped startup should notice that:

EMV is a long-run average; a player who can't survive the worst case can't reach the long run. Risk attitude is a business judgment, not math.

Q2. Perfect information is worth EV-with-crystal-ball ($40k) minus best EMV ($34k) = $6k. A $15k market study promising certainty is:

EVPI caps what ANY information can be worth; $15k > $6k fails the cap before you even ask how good the study is.

Q3. Studying only successful franchise owners to learn 'what works' repeats which famous error?

The owners who did the same things and failed are the planes that didn't come back — invisible, and decisive.
