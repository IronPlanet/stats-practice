ANOVA & MANOVA

Fisher’s wheat plots, Tukey’s honest differences, Wilks’s Λ — split the variation, compute F, then say which groups differ and by how much. Every symbol is defined before it is used; one worked example runs through every step.

1 · Where it came from

Rothamsted Experimental Station, England, 1919. Ronald Fisher is hired to make sense of seventy years of wheat-yield records from the Broadbalk field — plots with different fertilisers, different years, different weather. Comparing the plots two at a time would take hundreds of separate tests, and each test carries its own 5 % chance of a false alarm. Fisher’s idea was to stop comparing pairs of means and instead split the variation: the total variation in the yields is the sum of the variation between the treatment groups and the variation within them. If the treatments do nothing, those two pieces — each divided by its degrees of freedom — are about the same size, and their ratio, which he called F, is close to 1. He named the method the analysis of variance (ANOVA), published it in Statistical Methods for Research Workers (1925), and it became the standard tool for every field experiment, clinical trial and multi-group business test since.

Two extensions complete the story. John Tukey (1949) answered the question “F says something differs — but which groups?” with the Honestly Significant Difference (HSD), a way to compare every pair of groups while holding the overall false-alarm rate at 5 %. Samuel Wilks (1932) generalised the whole method to several outcomes measured at once — MANOVA, with his statistic Λ (lambda) as the test statistic — for the case where a treatment moves revenue and margin together and neither shows it alone.

2 · The mathematics, built from the beginning

This section builds one-way ANOVA in the order you would actually carry it out: name the quantities, state the hypotheses, check the assumptions, split the variation, compute F, read the p-value, report the effect size, then find which groups differ. Two-factor ANOVA and MANOVA follow, using the same building blocks. A small worked example runs through every step.

2.1 The setting and the notation

One-way ANOVA has one factor (a categorical variable such as service tier or buying rule) with k levels, called groups, and one numeric outcome (revenue, cost, wait time). Every symbol used below is defined here first.

Worked example (used in every step). Three groups of three observations each, so k = 3, ng = 3, N = 9.
Group 1: 4, 6, 8  →  x̄1 = 6   ·   Group 2: 5, 7, 9  →  x̄2 = 7   ·   Group 3: 8, 10, 12  →  x̄3 = 10   ·   grand mean x̄ = (6 + 7 + 10) / 3 = 7.667 (the groups are equal in size, so the mean of the group means is the grand mean).

2.2 Step 1 — the hypotheses

H0: μ1 = μ2 = … = μk      H1: at least one group mean is different from the others
μg (mu) is the population mean of group g — the true long-run average that x̄g estimates. The null hypothesis says all k population means are the same number. The alternative is deliberately vague: it does not say which mean differs, or by how much. That is why a significant result has to be followed by Step 7. Note that H1 is not “all the means differ”; one group standing apart from the rest is enough.

2.3 Step 2 — the assumptions that make F valid

F is a ratio of two variance estimates; the reference distribution for that ratio is exact only when three conditions hold. Check them before reading a p-value, because the fixes change which test you run.

AssumptionWhat it meansHow to checkIf it fails
IndependenceEach observation is a separate unit; no unit appears in two groups; observations inside a group do not share a hidden source (the same day, the same machine)The design — nothing in the data can check itNothing fixes it afterwards; aggregate to the unit that is independent (for example, one average per server instead of one row per day) and test that
Approximate normality within each groupInside each group the values are roughly bell-shaped, so the group mean is a good summaryShapiro–Wilk test per group; a histogram or Q–Q plot per groupMild departure with ng ≥ 30: proceed. Severe skew or outliers with small groups: use Kruskal–Wallis, the rank-based version
Equal variances across groups (homogeneity of variance)The spread inside each group is about the same, so one pooled “within” variance describes all of themLevene’s test (compares the groups’ |x − median| values; robust to non-normality); as a rough rule, the largest SD less than twice the smallestUse Welch’s ANOVA (the k-group version of Welch’s t-test) for the overall test, and Games–Howell instead of Tukey for the pairs. The failure matters most when the group sizes are unequal or one group’s variance is far larger than the rest

2.4 Step 3 — split the total variation into two pieces

The quantity being split is a sum of squares (SS): a sum of squared distances from a mean. Three sums of squares are needed.

SStotal  =  Σg Σi (xig − x̄)2     — every observation’s squared distance from the grand mean, added up

SSbetween  =  Σg ng (x̄g − x̄)2     — each group mean’s squared distance from the grand mean, weighted by the group’s size

SSwithin  =  Σg Σi (xig − x̄g)2     — every observation’s squared distance from its own group’s mean, added up
SStotal  =  SSbetween  +  SSwithin
This identity is exact, always — it is algebra, not an approximation. SSbetween is the part of the variation that the grouping explains (the groups have different means); SSwithin is the part it does not explain (values scatter around their own group mean for reasons the factor cannot account for), which is why it is also called the error or residual sum of squares.

Worked example. SSbetween = 3(6 − 7.667)² + 3(7 − 7.667)² + 3(10 − 7.667)² = 8.333 + 1.333 + 16.333 = 26.0.
SSwithin: group 1 gives (4 − 6)² + (6 − 6)² + (8 − 6)² = 8; groups 2 and 3 also give 8 each; total 24.0.
SStotal = 26.0 + 24.0 = 50.0 (check by direct calculation: Σ(xig − 7.667)² = 50.0).

2.5 Step 4 — degrees of freedom and mean squares

A sum of squares grows with the number of terms in it, so each one is divided by its degrees of freedom (df) — the number of values that were free to vary once the means used in the sum are fixed. The result is a mean square (MS), which is a variance.

SourceSum of squaresDegrees of freedomMean squareWhy that df
Between groupsSSbetweendfbetween = k − 1MSbetween = SSbetween / (k − 1)k group means, one constraint (they average to the grand mean)
Within groups (error)SSwithindfwithin = N − kMSwithin = SSwithin / (N − k)N observations, k constraints (each group’s values average to its own mean)
TotalSStotaldftotal = N − 1(not used)N observations, one constraint (the grand mean); and (k − 1) + (N − k) = N − 1
MSwithin is the pooled variance of the groups — the same quantity the pooled two-sample t-test uses — and it estimates the variance of the outcome when the factor has no effect. MSbetween estimates that same variance plus a term that grows with how far apart the population means are. Under H0 the extra term is zero and the two mean squares estimate the same number.

Worked example. dfbetween = 3 − 1 = 2; dfwithin = 9 − 3 = 6. MSbetween = 26.0 / 2 = 13.0; MSwithin = 24.0 / 6 = 4.0.

2.6 Step 5 — the F statistic and its p-value

F  =  MSbetweenMSwithin  =  SSbetween / (k − 1)SSwithin / (N − k)      compared with the F distribution with (k − 1, N − k) degrees of freedom
F is the ratio of the two variances. When H0 is true, F is close to 1 (both mean squares estimate the same variance, so their ratio scatters around 1). When the group means are far apart, MSbetween is inflated and F is much larger than 1. F can never be negative, and the test is always one-sided to the right: only large F counts against H0.

The p-value is the probability, if H0 were true, of an F at least this large: p = P(Fk−1, N−k ≥ Fobserved). In Excel: =F.DIST.RT(F, k−1, N−k). Decision rule: p < α (usually 0.05) → reject H0 and conclude that at least one group mean differs. The two df values are always reported with F, in the form F(k − 1, N − k).

Worked example. F = 13.0 / 4.0 = 3.25 on (2, 6) df; p = F.DIST.RT(3.25, 2, 6) = 0.111. Fail to reject H0 at α = 0.05: with only three values per group, these means are not far enough apart, relative to the scatter inside the groups, to rule out chance.

Special case: with k = 2, one-way ANOVA is exactly the pooled two-sample t-test, and F = t².

2.7 Step 6 — effect size: how much of the variation the grouping explains

η2  =  SSbetweenSStotal
η² (eta squared) is the fraction of the total sum of squares that sits between the groups — the share of the outcome’s variation that knowing the group accounts for. It runs from 0 (the groups explain nothing) to 1 (every value equals its group mean). Conventional labels: 0.01 small · 0.06 medium · 0.14 large. In a two-factor model the same ratio is computed for each term (Step 8). ω² (omega squared) is a corrected version that is less optimistic in small samples; report either, but say which.

Worked example. η² = 26.0 / 50.0 = 0.52. The grouping accounts for half of the variation — a large effect — even though the p-value was 0.111. Significance and size are different questions: with nine observations the test lacks power, but the pattern in the means is large. Report both numbers, always.

2.8 Step 7 — after a significant F: which groups differ?

F answers only “are they all the same?”. To say which pairs differ, every pair of groups has to be compared. The number of pairs is

m  =  k(k − 1) / 2     (k = 3 → 3 pairs; k = 4 → 6; k = 6 → 15)
Why not m ordinary t-tests? Each t-test run at α = 0.05 has a 5 % chance of a false alarm. Run m of them and the chance that at least one is a false alarm — the family-wise error rate — is 1 − (1 − α)m: 14 % for three pairs, 26 % for six, 54 % for fifteen. Two repairs exist. Bonferroni divides α by m and uses the smaller threshold for every pair (simple, but it discards power). Tukey’s HSD holds the family at 5 % with more power, by asking a sharper question: how large a gap between the largest and smallest of k sample means would chance alone produce?
Tukey HSD: groups a and b differ if   |x̄a − x̄b|  >  qα; k, N−k · √( MSwithin / n )
    (unequal group sizes — the Tukey–Kramer form:   qα; k, N−k · √( (MSwithin / 2) · (1/na + 1/nb) ))
qα; k, N−k is the critical value of the Studentized range distribution: the largest spread among k sample means, measured in standard-error units, that would occur by chance with probability α when H0 is true. It depends on k (more groups → larger expected spread) and on dfwithin = N − k. √(MSwithin / n) is the standard error of one group mean, using the pooled variance from the ANOVA table. Their product is the critical gap (the HSD): any pair whose observed gap exceeds it is declared different, and the whole set of m comparisons together keeps a 5 % family-wise error rate.

Worked example. q0.05; 3, 6 = 4.34 (from a Studentized-range table; Python: studentized_range.ppf(0.95, 3, 6)). Critical gap = 4.34 × √(4.0 / 3) = 5.01. The largest observed gap is x̄3 − x̄1 = 10 − 6 = 4.0, which is below 5.01, so no pair is declared different — consistent with the non-significant F.

When the variances are unequal (Step 2, Levene fails), Tukey’s critical gap is wrong, because MSwithin pools variances that are not equal: one very noisy group inflates the pooled variance and hides real gaps between the quiet groups. Use Games–Howell, which applies Tukey’s q with each pair’s own separate-variance standard error (the Welch idea), or Welch t-tests with a Bonferroni α. A significant ANOVA without a post-hoc comparison is an unfinished analysis.

2.9 Step 8 — two factors at once: two-way ANOVA and the interaction

Now there are two categorical factors — call them A with a levels and B with b levels — and every combination of a level of A with a level of B is a cell. With the same number of observations n in every cell the design is balanced and the arithmetic is clean. The total variation now splits four ways.

SStotal  =  SSA  +  SSB  +  SSA×B  +  SSwithin
TermWhat it measuresdfF-test
SSA — main effect of Ahow far the a level-means of A (averaged over all levels of B) sit from the grand meana − 1MSA / MSwithin
SSB — main effect of Bthe same for the b level-means of Bb − 1MSB / MSwithin
SSA×B — interactionhow far each cell mean sits from where the two main effects alone would put it(a − 1)(b − 1)MSA×B / MSwithin
SSwithin — errorscatter of the observations around their own cell meanab(n − 1)—
A main effect is the effect of one factor averaged over the levels of the other. The interaction is the part of the cell means that the two main effects added together cannot reproduce: it is non-zero when the effect of A is different at different levels of B. On an interaction plot (levels of A on the horizontal axis, one line per level of B, cell means as the points) parallel lines mean no interaction; lines with different slopes mean interaction; lines that cross mean the direction of A’s effect reverses.

Reading order. Test the interaction first. If it is significant, the main effects are no longer the story — an average effect of A that mixes a positive effect under B1 with a negative one under B2 describes no real situation — and the report gives the effect of A at each level of B. If the interaction is not significant, the two main effects can be read on their own. Each term gets its own η² = SSterm / SStotal; software also reports partial η² = SSterm / (SSterm + SSwithin), which is larger because the other terms are set aside — say which one you are quoting.

2.10 Step 9 — several outcomes at once: MANOVA and Wilks’ Λ

Everything so far has one numeric outcome. When there are p outcomes measured on the same units (revenue and margin; cost and wait time), each group has a vector of p means instead of a single mean, and the question becomes whether the groups differ on the combination. Two things replace their one-outcome versions.

One outcome (ANOVA)p outcomes (MANOVA)Meaning
SSwithin (a number)W — the within-group matrix (p × p)on its diagonal, SSwithin for each outcome; off the diagonal, the within-group cross-products Σ(x − x̄g)(y − ȳg), which carry how the outcomes move together inside groups
SSbetween (a number)B — the between-group matrix (p × p)the same construction for the group means around the grand means
SStotal = SSbetween + SSwithinT = B + Wthe identity still holds, matrix by matrix
Λ  =  |W||B + W|      0 ≤ Λ ≤ 1   ·   small Λ → reject H0
|W| and |B + W| are the determinants of the two matrices — a determinant of a sums-of-squares matrix is a generalised variance, a single number that measures the volume of scatter in all p dimensions at once. So Wilks’ Λ is the share of the total multivariate variation that is not explained by the grouping. With one outcome the matrices are 1 × 1, the determinants are the plain sums of squares, and Λ = SSwithin / SStotal = 1 − η². That is the way to read it: Λ close to 1 means the groups explain almost nothing; Λ close to 0 means they explain almost everything.

H0: the k groups have the same vector of population means (all p means equal across groups at once). H1: at least one group’s mean vector differs. Software converts Λ into an F statistic with a p-value (an exact conversion for p ≤ 2 or k ≤ 3, an approximation otherwise) and also prints Pillai’s trace, the most robust of the alternatives when the assumptions are shaky. With exactly two groups the test is Hotelling’s T², the p-outcome version of the two-sample t-test.

Why not one ANOVA per outcome? Two reasons. (1) p outcomes means p tests, and the family-wise error rate rises just as in Step 7. (2) Correlated outcomes carry joint information: two groups can be alike on revenue alone and alike on margin alone, yet clearly different on the combination — one earns more revenue at a lower margin — and only a test that uses the cross-products in W and B can see it. Follow-up. After a significant Λ, run the univariate ANOVAs (and Tukey) to say which outcomes carry the difference; if none is significant on its own, the finding is the joint pattern, and the report says so.

8 · When to reach for it — and when not

ANOVA / MANOVA when…Something else when…
One categorical factor with 3+ levels (tiers, regions, price points, training programs) and a numeric outcomeTwo groups → t-test (same answer, F = t²). Two categorical variables → χ²
Two factors and you suspect they interact (price × channel) → two-way ANOVAThe “factor” is numeric (spend, age) → regression (ANOVA is regression with dummy variables)
Several correlated outcomes that move together (revenue & margin) → MANOVA, then univariate follow-upsOutcomes are unrelated — separate ANOVAs with a Bonferroni α are simpler and clearer
Groups roughly Normal (or n ≥ 30 each) with similar spreads — or Welch ANOVA if spreads differSmall groups with heavy skew or outliers → Kruskal–Wallis
Three things a significant F does not tell you. Which groups differ (Tukey does). How big the difference is (η² and the gaps in real units do). Whether it will hold when you act on it — the data describe the groups you had, under the conditions you had.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. Three service tiers, monthly revenue: F(2, 297) = 6.4, p = 0.002. The analyst writes “the tiers differ; invest in the highest-revenue tier.” What is missing?

F says at least one mean differs; it does not say which. Pairwise t-tests inflate the family-wise error (14 % for three pairs); Tukey holds it at 5 %.

Q2. Six groups, all pairwise t-tests at α = 0.05, nothing is really going on. Probability of at least one “significant” pair?

k(k−1)/2 = 15 comparisons. The family-wise rate compounds. This is the argument for ANOVA + Tukey (or Bonferroni α/15).

Q3. MANOVA on revenue and margin across three tiers: Wilks’ Λ = 0.91, p = 0.01. The separate ANOVAs: revenue p = 0.09, margin p = 0.12. What do you report?

Correlated outcomes carry joint information. Groups can sit off the shared trend without differing on either axis. That trade-off is precisely what MANOVA is for.

Q4. Two-way ANOVA: price level × channel. The interaction is significant (p = 0.003); the main effect of price is not (p = 0.4). The recommendation should:

A significant interaction means the effect of one factor depends on the other. Averaging a positive and a negative effect gives a null main effect that describes no real channel.
