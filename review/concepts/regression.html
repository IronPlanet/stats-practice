Regression

Fitting a line, reading a slope in real units, and what changes the moment a second predictor joins the model.

1 · Where it came from

The method came first, the name came later, and the name is an accident. Legendre published least squares in 1805 and Gauss claimed he had been using it since 1795; both were fitting orbits, not business data.

The word “regression” is Francis Galton's, from the 1880s. Measuring heights of parents and children, he found tall parents had tall children — but slightly less tall, closer to the average. He called it “regression toward mediocrity”. He thought he had found a law of heredity. He had actually found a mathematical inevitability of imperfect correlation, which shows up any time you measure something twice with noise. Sales teams rediscover it every quarter when the top performer slips and the bottom one improves, and every quarter someone credits the coaching.

2 · The mathematics

ŷ = b0 + b1x      b1 = ∑(xi−x̄)(yi−ȳ)∑(xi−x̄)2      b0 = ȳ − b1x̄
The line is chosen to make the sum of squared vertical distances as small as possible. Squared, so that misses above and below cannot cancel and a big miss counts far more than a small one. Vertical, because we are predicting y from x — swapping the roles gives a different line.
The line always passes through (x̄, ȳ). That falls straight out of the b0 formula, and it is a useful sanity check on any fit you are handed.
SST = SSR + SSE      R2 = SSRSST      R̄2 = 1 − (1−R2)n−1n−p
SST — total variation in y about its mean, the thing you are trying to explain. SSR — the part the line explains. SSE — what is left over. R̄2 (adjusted) charges you for each predictor, so unlike R2 it can fall when you add a useless variable. That asymmetry is what makes it the honest one to quote when comparing models of different sizes.
se = √ SSE / (n − p)       F = SSR / kSSE / (n−p)      tj = bjSE(bj)
se, the standard error of the estimate, is typical prediction error in the units of y — dollars, minutes, units. It is the number a manager actually needs, and it is the one nobody quotes. F asks whether the predictors as a set beat an empty model; tj asks about one predictor with the others held fixed. They can disagree, and that disagreement is informative.

Say the slope out loud in real units. b₁ = 0.949 means each additional weekly usage hour is associated with about $0.95 more monthly maintenance cost. Not “causes”. Not “0.949 units”. A coefficient a manager cannot repeat back in their own words has not been reported.
And be careful with b₀. Here the intercept is 10.78 — the predicted cost of a machine used zero hours a week. The data runs from 10 to 40 hours, so x = 0 is outside it entirely. The number is a mathematical anchor for the line, not a business quantity.
r² = 0.8656 and R² = 0.8656 — identical, as they must be with one predictor. That equality stops holding the moment you add a second.

5 · Adding a predictor — what changes

Now add machine age in years. Same twelve rows, one more column.

Age earns nothing, and the table says so three ways. Its p-value is 0.80 — no evidence of an effect once hours is in the model. R² rose from 0.8656 to 0.8666, a gain of 0.0010, which is the best R² can ever do: it cannot fall when you add a variable, however useless. And adjusted R² fell, from 0.8521 to 0.8369. That fall is the honest verdict. se rose too, from 3.827 to 4.019 — the model predicts worse in dollars after the addition.
But look what happened to hours anyway. It moved from 0.949 to 1.055 — an 11% shift caused by adding a variable that contributes nothing. Nothing about the machines changed; the question changed. The simple model asks “how does cost differ between machines used more hours?” The multiple model asks “… used more hours but the same age?” That is ceteris paribus, and it is why a coefficient can never be quoted without naming every other variable in the model.
Comparing predictors needs standardised coefficients. Raw b values live on different scales — one per hour, one per year — so they cannot be ranked. Multiply each by sx/sy: hours gives β = 1.034, age gives β = -0.109. Both now read in standard deviations, and the comparison confirms what the p-values said — hours carries the model and age is noise around zero.
The rule this demonstrates. Never judge whether a predictor belongs by watching R². It is structurally incapable of arguing against a variable. Use adjusted R², se, or the predictor's own p-value — all three agree here, and all three disagree with R².

6 · Categorical predictors

A category enters as a 0/1 dummy. Its coefficient is the mean shift relative to a reference group — the category coded 0 — holding everything else constant. “Region B: +$412” is meaningless until you say relative to which region. With k categories you create k−1 dummies; including all k makes the columns add up to the intercept and the model will not estimate.
Naming the reference group is not a formality. Every categorical coefficient in the report is a comparison against it. A table of dummy coefficients with no stated baseline cannot be interpreted by the reader, and usually was not interpreted by the author either.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. Adding a second predictor lifts R² from 0.8656 to 0.8666, but adjusted R² falls from 0.8521 to 0.8369 and the new predictor's p-value is 0.80. Should it stay in the model?

R² is structurally incapable of arguing against a predictor: adding any column, even random noise, can only raise it or leave it unchanged. Adjusted R² charges for the extra parameter and fell, sₑ rose, and p = 0.80. Three honest measures agree; only the one that cannot disagree said otherwise.

Q2. A model reports R² = 0.91 and sₑ = $4.80 on an outcome averaging $32. Which sentence is most useful to a manager?

R² is a share of variance — unitless, and easy to inflate. sₑ is in dollars, and answers the question the manager actually has: how far off will this be? A high R² also never validates a model; that is what residual diagnostics are for.

Q3. A model has dummies for Region B, C and D, with coefficients +412, +180 and −95. What is missing?

Each coefficient is a difference from the omitted category. Without naming it, “+412” has no meaning. Adding the fourth dummy is the opposite of the fix — it makes the model unestimable.

Q4. A model reports b = 1.05 for usage hours and b = −0.40 for machine age in years. A colleague concludes hours matters more because 1.05 > 0.40. What is wrong with that reasoning?

One is dollars per hour, the other dollars per year — changing age to months would multiply its coefficient by twelve without changing anything real. Multiply each by sₓ/sₖ to put both in standard deviations. Magnitude and significance are also separate questions: a p-value ranks evidence that an effect is non-zero, not how big it is.
