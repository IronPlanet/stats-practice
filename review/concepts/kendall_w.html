Kendall’s W — the Coefficient of Concordance

Kendall & Babington Smith’s 1939 question — when several judges rank the same things, how much do they really agree? A number from 0 to 1, and a warning about trusting the p-value instead.

1 · Where it came from

1939. Maurice Kendall — a British statistician who had spent the decade on rank correlation (his τ, 1938) — and Bernard Babington Smith asked a question that every hiring panel, wine competition, and grant committee still asks: when several judges rank the same set of things, how much do they actually agree? Not “do judge 1 and judge 2 agree” (that is a correlation) but the whole panel at once. Their answer, the coefficient of concordance W, runs from 0 (the rankings are unrelated — the panel might as well have drawn lots) to 1 (every judge produced the identical order).

The idea is elegant. Add up each item’s ranks across the judges. If the judges agree, the favourites collect small totals and the also-rans collect large ones — the totals spread out. If the judges disagree, every item gets a mix of high and low ranks and the totals all land near the same middling value. W is simply how spread out the totals are, as a fraction of the maximum spread possible. It is also, exactly, the average Spearman correlation between all pairs of judges, rescaled — which is why it is the multi-rater cousin of ρ. (Kendall’s τ is a different thing: a correlation between two rankings, not a concordance measure. Do not let a tool conflate them.)

2 · The mathematics

The question it answers. m raters each rank the same n items. Is the panel’s agreement strong enough that the combined ranking means something?
Step 1   build the m × n matrix of ranks (rater in rows, item in columns; each row is a permutation of 1 … n)
Step 2   Ri = column total for item i     R̄ = m(n+1)/2 (every column total averages this)
Step 3   S = Σi (Ri − R̄)2    (the spread of the totals)
Step 4   W = 12 Sm2(n3 − n)    (the denominator is the largest S possible — perfect agreement)
Step 5   significance: χ² = m(n − 1) W on df = n − 1   (n > 7; exact tables for smaller n)
H0: no agreement — each rater’s ranking is an independent random permutation · H1: the rankings are associated · W — 0 … 1, unit-free · ties: use average ranks and subtract a tie correction from the denominator (software does it) · W and Spearman: the mean of all pairwise ρ’s equals (mW − 1)/(m − 1).

Why the maximum S is m²(n³ − n)/12

With perfect agreement item i gets rank i from every rater, so Ri = m·i and the totals are m, 2m, …, nm. Their variance about the mean is m² times the variance of 1 … n, which is (n² − 1)/12 per item; summed over n items: m²·n(n² − 1)/12 = m²(n³ − n)/12. Any disagreement pulls totals toward the middle and shrinks S. W = S / Smax.

Reading W — the value first, the p-value second

WAgreementBusiness reading
< 0.3weakthe combined ranking is mostly noise; the raters evaluated different things
0.3 – 0.5moderatea shared signal exists but individual raters diverge a lot; use with caution, look at who disagrees
0.5 – 0.7strongthe combined ranking is reliable for the top and bottom; the middle may be blurry
> 0.7very strongact on the combined ranking
Significant is not the same as reliable. The χ² test asks whether W differs from zero. With ten items and five raters, W = 0.25 gives χ² = 11.25 on 9 df, p ≈ 0.26 — not even significant; W = 0.40 gives χ² = 18, p ≈ 0.035 — “significant” and still only moderate. With 30 items and 12 raters, W = 0.2 is significant at p < 0.0001 and still weak. The number to act on is W.

5 · Does the test fit? — assumptions and the exact-p question

AssumptionHow to checkIf it fails
Every rater ranks every item (complete rankings)No blanks in the matrixMissing cells → drop the item or rater; partial rankings need other methods
Rankings, not scoresEach row is a permutation of 1 … nScores (1–10 ratings) → convert each rater’s scores to ranks first (ties → average ranks + tie correction)
Raters judged independentlyDesign — no discussion before rankingDiscussion before ranking inflates W: you are measuring persuasion, not agreement
n > 7 for the χ² approximationCountn ≤ 7 → exact critical values of S (Kendall’s table) or a permutation test
Relatives. Two raters → Spearman’s ρ. Raters scoring rather than ranking, with agreement in level mattering → intraclass correlation (ICC). Two raters assigning categories → Cohen’s κ. Same units ranked under several conditions → Friedman’s test — which is algebraically the same χ² as Kendall’s W with the roles of rows and columns swapped.

6 · When to reach for it — and when not

Kendall’s W when…Something else when…
A panel ranks candidates, vendors, projects, wines — and you must decide whether the aggregate ranking is trustworthy before actingOnly two raters → Spearman ρ (same information, familiar scale)
You want a single reliability number for the whole process, not pairwise comparisonsRaters give scores and the absolute level matters (is 8/10 the same 8 for everyone?) → ICC
Prioritisation exercises: which of n features does the team agree matters most?Categorical judgements (approve / reject) → Cohen’s or Fleiss’s κ
Auditing an evaluation process — low W is a process defect, not a data quirkYou need to know which rater is the outlier → drop-one-rater W, or pairwise ρ matrix
Three mistakes. (1) Reporting p without W — with many items a weak W is “significant”. (2) Treating Kendall’s W and Kendall’s τ as the same thing — τ is a two-variable correlation. (3) Computing W on scores without ranking each rater’s row first.
Reporting line. “Kendall’s W = 0.85 (very strong), m = 4 raters, n = 6 candidates, χ²(5) = 17.0, p = 0.0045; combined ranking 1 > 2 > 3 > 4 > 5 > 6.” Then what it cannot say: whether the agreement reflects merit or a shared bias.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. Five interviewers rank ten candidates: W = 0.22, χ²(9) = 9.9, p = 0.36. The Director asks whether to hire the top-ranked candidate.

W = 0.22 is weak and the test can’t reject zero agreement. The raters evaluated different things. Acting on the aggregate would be acting on noise.

Q2. Thirty products ranked by twelve buyers: W = 0.24, χ²(29) = 83.5, p < 0.0001. What is the honest summary?

p tests W ≠ 0, and n = 30 makes that easy. W is the reliability number; 0.24 says the buyers mostly disagree.

Q3. Four raters’ column totals over five items are 12, 12, 12, 12, 12. W = ?

R̄ = m(n+1)/2 = 12 for every item means no spread at all. That happens when rankings are unrelated (or exactly contradictory).

Q4. A tool reports “Kendall = 0.61” for a five-rater panel. What must you check before reporting it as concordance?

Kendall’s τ compares two rankings; W summarises m ≥ 2 rankings at once. A τ between two raters says nothing about the panel.
