Regression Diagnostics

Anscombe's warning made practical: the residual checks that catch what R² cannot see.

1 · Where it came from

In 1973 the statistician Francis Anscombe published four small datasets. All four have the same mean x, the same mean y, the same slope, the same intercept, and the same R2 to two decimals. Plotted, they could not be more different: one is a clean line, one is a perfect curve, one is a line plus a single wild point, and one is a vertical stack of identical x values with one lone observation setting the entire slope.

Anscombe's point was blunt and has not aged: summary statistics do not describe a dataset. R2 cannot tell you whether the relationship is a line, whether one observation is running the show, or whether the error grows with the prediction. Only the residuals can. R. Dennis Cook supplied the measure for the third case in 1977 — Cook's distance, which asks how much the whole fitted line moves if you delete one point.

2 · The mathematics

ei = yi − ŷi      hi = 1n + (xi − x̄)2∑(xj − x̄)2
ei — the residual, how far this observation sits from the line. hi — the leverage, how unusual this observation's x value is. Leverage knows nothing about y. A rule of thumb flags hi above 2p/n, here 0.167.
Di = ei2p se2 · hi(1 − hi)2      VIFj = 11 − Rj2
Cook's D multiplies the two ideas: a point is influential only if it is both unusual in x and far from the line. VIF comes from regressing predictor j on all the others — if they predict it well, Rj2 approaches 1 and the VIF explodes.
Three different things, routinely confused. An outlier has a large residual. A high-leverage point has an extreme x. An influential point is both, and only the third one moves your conclusions. A dramatic outlier sitting at the middle of the x range often changes the slope not at all.

4 · Excel lab — residuals, VIF, and a variance test

Back to the hours-and-age data from the Regression page.

Residuals. Fit with Data → Data Analysis → Regression and tick Residuals and Residual Plots. Or by hand: predicted in C2 as =$F$2+$F$3*A2, residual in D2 as =B2-C2. The residuals must sum to zero — =SUM(D2:D13) gives 0.00, which is a check on your arithmetic, not evidence of a good model.

What you wantFormulaYou should see
R² of age on hours=RSQ(AGE,HOURS)0.9141
VIF for either predictor=1/(1-RSQ(AGE,HOURS))11.65
Breusch–Pagan: R² of e² on hours=RSQ(SQ_RESID,HOURS)0.0714
BP statistic n·R²=12*RSQ(...)0.857
BP p-value=CHISQ.DIST.RT(stat,1)0.3547
Reading the VIF. 11.65 is unremarkable — the usual alarm is 5, and 10 is serious. Contrast it with the wage-prediction case from Week 4, where AGE was literally EDU + EXP + 6. That is an accounting identity, not a correlation, and it drove the VIF to 288. No amount of data fixes that; one of the three variables has to go, because it contains no information the other two lack.
Reading the BP test. p = 0.3547 here, so there is no evidence the error variance changes with x. Had it been small, the coefficients would stay unbiased but their standard errors — and therefore every p-value and interval — would be wrong. The fix is robust (HC3) standard errors, not deleting anything.

5 · Four traps the residuals catch and R² does not

Suppression. A predictor with almost no correlation to the outcome on its own can be strongly significant once a correlated covariate is in the model — it was carrying noise that the covariate now absorbs. The practical consequence: you cannot screen predictors by their individual correlation with y and drop the weak ones. And you cannot use that variable alone as a proxy later.
Extrapolation. The model knows nothing outside the x range it saw. Predicting maintenance for a machine run 90 hours a week, when the data tops out at 40, is not a cautious estimate — it is an invented number, and a high R² does nothing to protect it. Always state the range the model is valid over.
Curvature. A residual plot that smiles or frowns means the relationship is not a line. R² can still be high while every prediction at the ends is systematically wrong in the same direction.
Non-normal residuals. Check with =SKEW() on the residual column, a histogram, or Shapiro–Wilk. It matters most below about n = 30; in large samples the t and F tests are fairly forgiving. Strong skew usually points at a missing predictor or a needed transformation rather than at the errors themselves.

Shape first. There is no point testing the variance of residuals from a model that is the wrong functional form.

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. An observation has a very large residual but sits in the middle of the x range. Removing it will:

Influence needs both an extreme x and a large residual. A point at the centre of x has low leverage, so it pulls the line up or down slightly but cannot pivot it. It does inflate sₑ and depress R², which is why the fit looks better without it.

Q2. A wage model includes AGE, EDUCATION and EXPERIENCE, where AGE = EDUCATION + EXPERIENCE + 6 by construction. The VIF is 288. The fix is:

This is an exact identity, not a large correlation. The third variable is arithmetically determined by the other two, so no sample size helps and no standard-error adjustment addresses it. Robust SEs fix non-constant variance, a different problem entirely.

Q3. Breusch–Pagan returns p = 0.008. What is now wrong with your regression output?

Non-constant error variance leaves the coefficients unbiased but makes the usual standard-error formula wrong, so the inference built on it is unreliable. Robust (HC3) standard errors repair it without changing a single coefficient.

Q4. A model fitted on machines run 12 to 40 hours a week is used to predict cost at 90 hours. R² is 0.94. How much should you trust the prediction?

R² measures fit inside the observed range. Beyond it the model has no evidence at all — the relationship could bend, saturate or reverse, and nothing in the output would warn you. State the valid range with every model you hand over.
