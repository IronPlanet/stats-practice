The Exponential Distribution

Erlang's telephone-exchange math — the clock on the gap between random events, joined at the hip to the Poisson, and famously without a memory.

1 · Where it came from

The exponential grew up inside early telephone engineering. Around 1909, A. K. Erlang — a Danish mathematician working for the Copenhagen Telephone Company — needed to know how long until the next call arrives, so the exchange could be staffed. His answer built queueing theory, and the waiting-time law at its heart is the exponential distribution.

It is the Poisson’s partner, joined at the hip: Poisson counts the events per window; exponential times the wait between them. Same process, two questions. Calls per hour is Poisson; minutes until the next call is exponential.

2 · The mathematics

f(x) = λ e−λx    ·    P(X ≤ x) = 1 − e−λx
λ (lambda) — the event rate per unit time (same λ as the Poisson!) · x — the waiting time · mean wait = 1/λ (rate 0.25/min ⇒ average 4-minute gap)
Mean = 1/λ   ·   SD = 1/λ
Signature — memoryless. Given you’ve already waited 5 minutes, the chance of waiting 2 more is EXACTLY the same as it was at the start: P(X > s+t | X > s) = P(X > t). The process doesn’t age. No other continuous distribution has this property — if history changes the odds (wear-out, fatigue), the exponential is the wrong model.
Second signature — mean equals SD. If your waiting-time data has mean 5.1 and SD 5.0, the exponential is knocking. Mean 5.1, SD 1.2? That’s a scheduled process, not a random one.

3 · What it looks like — drag the rate

P(wait ≤ x) — shaded:  =EXPON.DIST(2, 0.25, TRUE)  → 0.3935
No hump — the curve starts at its peak and decays. Short waits are always the most likely, yet long waits keep real probability: that's why queues feel unfair.

4 · Excel — run it in class

Scenario: Support calls hit a helpdesk at λ = 0.25 per minute (one every 4 minutes on average). Set up:

celltype thismeaningyou should see
B10.25rate λ per minute0.25
B3=EXPON.DIST(2,B1,TRUE)P(next call within 2 min)0.3935
B4=1-EXPON.DIST(10,B1,TRUE)P(a quiet gap longer than 10 min)0.0821
B5=EXPON.DIST(4,B1,TRUE)P(within the average wait) — NOT 50%!0.6321
B6=1/B1mean wait4.0
The B5 surprise to spring on the class: 63.2% of waits finish before the “average” wait. The distribution is skewed — a few very long gaps drag the mean up past the typical experience. (That 0.6321 = 1 − 1/e appears for every λ. Ask them why.)
Excel trap: EXPON.DIST wants the RATE λ, not the mean. If you know the average gap (4 min), the argument is 1/4 = 0.25. Feeding it the 4 is the classic error — the answers come out absurdly high.

5 · Does real data fit?

How to check fit: mean ≈ SD is the entry ticket. Then overlay the fitted decay curve (λ = 1/mean) on the histogram: highest bar at zero, smooth decay, a long thin tail. A hump away from zero says “scheduled, not random.”

6 · When to reach for it — and when not

Reach for Exponential when…Do NOT use Exponential when…
Timing the wait until the NEXT independent event (calls, walk-ins, failures) The event wears in or wears out (machine aging) — memorylessness fails
The matching count-per-window is Poisson (same λ, two views) Arrivals are scheduled or batched (appointments, deliveries)
Sample mean ≈ sample SDSD ≪ mean (regular process) or bimodal gaps (two mixed processes)

✅ Self-check — no internet needed

Q1. Orders arrive as a Poisson stream. 'How long until the NEXT order?' is exponential because:

One process, two views: count the events (Poisson) or time the gaps (exponential). They're mathematically the same randomness seen from two angles

Q2. 'Memoryless' means the machine that hasn't failed in 6 months is no safer or riskier THIS hour than a fresh one. When is that assumption WRONG?

Exponential fits shock-driven failures (lightning, random defects) but not wear-out. If risk grows with age, the memoryless model understates old-equipment danger

Q3. HOW does a service manager use an exponential model with mean wait 10 minutes between walk-ins?

The exponential answers wait-time questions: P(next event within t), P(lull longer than t). Amounts, staff comparisons, and trends are other tools
