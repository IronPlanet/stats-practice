Which test? A field guide to t, Welch's t, F and χ²

Every one of these tests answers the same question: could this difference be nothing but luck? This page shows what each one measures, the math behind the score, and — most important — how to pick the right one.

1The one idea behind every test

You have two numbers that are different. The question is never "are they different?" — they always are. The question is "is the gap bigger than luck alone would produce?"

Every test on this page works the same way in three moves:

  1. Compute a score from the data. The score is always some version of signal ÷ noise: how big the gap is, divided by how much wobble you'd expect anyway.
  2. Hand the score to a referee. The referee is a curve (t, F or χ²) that says how often a score that big shows up when nothing real is going on.
  3. Read the p-value. That's the referee's answer: the fraction of "nothing going on" worlds that would produce a score at least this extreme. Small p (usually under 0.05) → luck is a poor explanation → we say the gap is statistically significant.

Same recipe every time. The only things that change are what kind of data you have (which sets the score) and which referee you hand it to.

Why "t" instead of the normal curve?

You already know the bell curve. A t-curve is a bell curve that has been told "you only saw a small sample, so be humble." With few observations, your estimate of the spread (the standard deviation) is shaky, so the t-curve grows fatter tails: extreme scores are treated as more ordinary, and you need a bigger score to be convinced. As the sample grows, t turns back into the normal curve.

2The map: which test, and why

Ask three questions about your data. The answers walk you to the test.

Click any box to jump to that section. Blue = comparing averages (t family). Orange = comparing spreads (F). Green = comparing counts (χ²).

Question 1 — What did you measure?

Numbers Seconds, dollars, degrees, scores. You will compare averages (t, or ANOVA for 3+ groups) or spreads (F).

Categories / counts Yes-or-no, weekday, which plan, complained-or-not. You count how many landed in each bucket and compare counts (χ²).

Question 2 — How many groups, and are they the same individuals twice?

One group vs a target number → one-sample t. The same stores/people measured before and after → paired t. Two separate groups → two-sample t. Three or more groups → ANOVA (which uses the F referee).

Question 3 — Do the two groups have the same spread?

This is where the F-test comes in. If the spreads are similar, the classic (pooled) t-test is fine. If one group is much wilder than the other, use Welch's t. When in doubt, Welch is always safe.

χ² has two jobs

Goodness of fit: do counts across buckets match a pattern you expected (equal, or proportional to something)? Association: are two category variables linked (does the service plan change the chance of a slow response)?

3The F-test — the spread check

Plain English. Two groups can have the same average and still behave very differently: one steady, one wild. The F-test asks whether two spreads are really different. It is also the engine inside ANOVA — but here we use it for its simplest job.

The score

Variance is the standard deviation squared (s²). The F score is just one variance divided by the other, bigger on top:

F  =  s2largers2smaller

If the two groups wobble the same amount, F lands near 1. The further above 1 it climbs, the harder it is to blame luck. Because F is a ratio of squared things it can never go below zero, so the referee curve is lopsided: a hump near 1 with a long tail to the right.

The referee

The F-curve needs two degrees of freedom — one for each group: df1 = n1 − 1 (top group), df2 = n2 − 1 (bottom group). Small groups → wide curve → you need a bigger F to convince it.

Plug in real numbers — a before/after example

A service desk measures its daily average wait. Before a process change: 14 days, SD = 4.80 s. After: 31 days, SD = 1.87 s.

  1. Square the SDs: 4.80² = 23.0 and 1.87² = 3.50.
  2. Bigger over smaller: F = 23.0 ÷ 3.50 = 6.6.
  3. Degrees of freedom: df1 = 14 − 1 = 13, df2 = 31 − 1 = 30.
  4. Referee: with (13, 30) df, a ratio this lopsided happens by luck about 2 times in 100,000 (p ≈ 0.00002, two-sided — the same number Excel's F.TEST reports).
Verdict: the spreads are different. the before period was six times wilder (in variance) than the after period. → Do not pool; use Welch's t for the averages.

Two honest caveats. First, the F-test for spreads is touchy about non-normal data — a couple of outliers can inflate a variance and fake a "different" verdict. Statisticians often prefer Levene's test for that reason, but the idea is identical. Second, when the data are not normal, the F-test may signal "different" when a rank-based test is the safer road — see Non-Parametric Methods.

4t-test vs Welch's t-test

The rule, up front. Both tests compare two averages. The only question that decides between them is: do the two groups have the same spread (variance)?

Check first with =F.TEST(a, b) (two-tailed, order doesn't matter) or scipy.stats.f.sf(F, df1, df2). Third argument in T.TEST = tails (2 = two-sided); fourth = type (1 paired · 2 equal variances · 3 unequal variances).

Plain English. Both tests compare two averages. The score is gap ÷ noise. They disagree only about how to measure the noise: the classic test assumes both groups wobble equally and blends their spreads; Welch's keeps them separate. If the spreads really are equal, the two tests give nearly the same answer. If they are not, the classic one can be over-confident.

The score (the part they share)

t  =  x̄1 − x̄2SE    gap between the averages ÷ standard error (the expected wobble of that gap)

The noise (the part they argue about)

Classic (pooled / Student's) t

Blend the two variances into one, weighting by sample size:

s2p = (n1−1)s12 + (n2−1)s22n1 + n2 − 2

SE = √( s2p · ( 1n1 + 1n2 ) )

df = n1 + n2 − 2. Simple, and the most power if the spreads truly match.

Welch's t

Keep each group's own variance, each divided by its own n:

SE = √( s12n1 + s22n2 )

The price: a smaller (and usually non-whole-number) df, computed by the Welch–Satterthwaite formula:

df = (a + b)2a2/(n1−1) + b2/(n2−1)
where a = s12/n1 and b = s22/n2 (the two pieces of Welch's SE)

Think of it as a penalty: the wilder, smaller group drags the df down, so you need a bigger t.

Plug in real numbers — the same before/after example

Before: n = 14, mean 37.26 s, SD 4.80. After: n = 31, mean 30.96 s, SD 1.87. Gap = 6.30 s.

Classic

  1. s²p = (13 × 23.0 + 30 × 3.50) ÷ 43 = (299.5 + 105) ÷ 43 = 9.41
  2. SE = √(9.41 × (1/14 + 1/31)) = √(9.41 × 0.1037) = 0.99
  3. t = 6.30 ÷ 0.99 = 6.38, df = 43 → p ≈ 0.0000001

Welch

  1. a = 23.0/14 = 1.646, b = 3.50/31 = 0.113
  2. SE = √(1.646 + 0.113) = √1.759 = 1.33
  3. t = 6.30 ÷ 1.33 = 4.75, df = (1.759)² ÷ (1.646²/13 + 0.113²/30) = 3.094 ÷ 0.2088 ≈ 14.8 → p ≈ 0.0003
Both reject "no difference" — but look at the noise. Classic says SE = 0.99; Welch says 1.33. The classic test under-counted the noise by a third because it let the calm "after" group vouch for the wild "before" group. Same conclusion this time; not always.

Recipe for a disagreement: make the small group the wild one (say n A = 5, SD A = 9, n B = 50, SD B = 1) and a modest gap of about 4. Classic t says "significant"; Welch says "not so fast". That is exactly the situation the classic test gets wrong.

So which do I use? Run the F-test (or just compare the SDs — a ratio above about 2 is a warning). Similar spreads → either test, they agree. Different spreads → Welch. And if you can only remember one rule: Welch is never wrong to use. Its only cost is a slightly smaller df, which barely matters unless the groups are tiny. Most modern software defaults to Welch for that reason.

5χ² — the test for counts

Plain English. No averages here. You sorted things into buckets and counted. The χ² (chi-square) test asks: are the counts you observed far from the counts you'd expect if nothing interesting were happening?

The score

χ2  =  Σ (Observed − Expected)2Expected

For every bucket: take the miss, square it (so misses in either direction count, and big misses count a lot), then divide by the expected count (a miss of 5 is a big deal when you expected 10, a rounding error when you expected 1,000). Add them all up. Zero means a perfect match; bigger means further from "boring".

The referee

The χ²-curve, with df = (number of buckets − 1) for a goodness-of-fit test, or df = (rows − 1) × (columns − 1) for a table. Like F, it lives only on the positive side with a right tail. One rule of thumb: every expected count should be at least 5, or the referee's answer gets unreliable.

Job 1 — Goodness of fit: "are complaints evenly spread across the week?"

Complaints logged at a help desk over one month, by weekday. Total 250. If weekday doesn't matter, we expect 250 ÷ 7 = 35.7 per day.

How χ² relates to the others. Notice the shape of the score: (miss)² ÷ expected, summed. That is a squared, standardised distance — the same idea as t² or the "between ÷ within" of F. All three referees are cousins: t² with 1 df on top is an F; a χ² with 1 df is a squared normal. Different data types, one family of ideas.

The flow in one breath. Counts → χ². Numbers → how many groups? One → one-sample t. Same things twice → paired t. Two groups → check spreads with F → similar: classic t, different: Welch. Three or more → ANOVA. Data badly skewed or tiny → swap the t for its rank-based cousin (Mann–Whitney, Wilcoxon) — see Non-Parametric Methods.

7Five mistakes that cost points (and money)

1
Using a two-sample t on paired data. If the same twelve stores were measured before and after, the before-and-after numbers travel together. The paired test removes the store-to-store noise; the two-sample test leaves it in and often finds "nothing" when something is there.
2
Pooling when the spreads are obviously different. A wild small group plus a calm big group makes the classic t over-confident. F-test first, or default to Welch.
3
Reading a tiny p-value as a big effect. p says "not luck". It says nothing about "how much". A 0.3-second improvement can be wildly significant with enough data and still be worthless. Report the gap (and Cohen's d) next to the p.
4
Running χ² on percentages or averages. χ² needs raw counts of things in buckets. 45 % is not a count; 45 out of 100 is.
5
Testing the wrong question. "Is the after period calmer than the before period?" is a spread question (F), not an average question (t). Write the question as a sentence before choosing the test.

8Self-check — no calculator needed

1. A manager compares average delivery time for two courier companies, 8 shipments each. One company's times range 20–24 min, the other's 10–45 min. Which test?

Welch's t. Two separate groups (two-sample), and the spreads are clearly unequal (an F-test would confirm it). Pooling would let the steady company hide the wild one's noise.

2. Same twelve branches, wait time measured the week before and the week after a software change. Which test?

Paired t on the twelve differences. The "before" and "after" numbers belong to the same branch, so you subtract first and test whether the average difference is zero.

3. Of 300 customers on the basic plan, 40 complained; of 200 on the premium plan, 12 complained. Is plan linked to complaining?

χ² association on the 2 × 2 table (plan × complained). Counts in buckets, two category variables. df = 1.

4. Group A has variance 20, group B has variance 5. What is F, and what does "F ≈ 1" mean?

F = 20 ÷ 5 = 4. F ≈ 1 would mean the two groups wobble about the same amount — no evidence of different spreads. Whether 4 is "big enough" depends on the sample sizes (the df).

5. Two tests on the same data: classic t gives p = 0.03, Welch gives p = 0.09. What happened, and which do you report?

The spreads (or sizes) differ enough that pooling under-counted the noise. Report Welch. The disagreement itself is the finding: the evidence is weaker than the classic test claimed.

6. Complaints by weekday: Mon 42, Tue 31, Wed 39, Thu 29, Fri 33, Sat 36, Sun 40. Your boss says "Mondays are clearly worse." What do you say?

Run χ² goodness of fit against an equal split (35.7 each). χ² ≈ 4.0 on 6 df, p ≈ 0.67 — this much day-to-day wobble is normal. Eyeballs convict on vibes; the referee wants evidence.
